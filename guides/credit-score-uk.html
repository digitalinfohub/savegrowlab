Your credit score: what actually moves it

The score is not a single number and it is not a verdict on you. It is a summary of a file held by three companies, scored differently by each, and interpreted differently by every lender. Once you know what goes into the file, the whole thing gets less mysterious — and much easier to fix.

What this guide covers

  • Why Experian, Equifax and TransUnion give you three different numbers.
  • The five things that move a file, ranked by how fast they work.
  • How credit utilisation is calculated, with the actual arithmetic.
  • Which searches lenders see, and how to remove errors from a report.

Three agencies, three scores

In the UK there are three credit reference agencies — Experian, Equifax and TransUnion — and they do not share data with each other. A mobile contract might only be reported to one. A lender might only search another. That is why your score can be "excellent" on one app and "fair" on another, and why the number on a free app is not the number a mortgage underwriter sees.

It helps to separate three things people use interchangeably:

  • Your credit file. The facts: accounts, balances, payment history, searches, court records. This is the part you can check and correct.
  • Your credit score. One agency's summary of that file. Useful as a trend, meaningless as an absolute.
  • A lender's decision. Their own model, their own affordability rules, and often a different agency's data. Two people with identical scores can get different answers.

You can see all three files for free: each agency offers a statutory report or a free account, and the statutory report costs £2 by post or is free online from some providers. Checking does not harm your file — self-checks are soft searches.

Financial associations are the quiet one. A joint account with an ex-partner, or a car loan with a friend, links your files. If their file has problems, yours carries the shadow. You can ask an agency to disassociate you once the account is closed — it takes a written request, not a phone call.

The five levers

Ranked by speed, from "visible this month" to "takes years":

  1. Register to vote at your current address. The single fastest improvement for most people, and free. If you cannot register — some visa holders cannot — ask the agency to add a notice of correction explaining it.
  2. Lower utilisation. Paying a card down before the statement date changes the reported balance in weeks. See the arithmetic below.
  3. Never miss a payment. One missed payment stays for six years. A direct debit for the minimum on every card removes the risk.
  4. Stop applying for things. Each hard search dents the file and clusters look like desperation. Space applications at least three months apart.
  5. Let accounts age. Average account age is a real factor. Closing your oldest card shortens it.

Utilisation, with numbers

Utilisation is the percentage of your available revolving credit that you are using. On a card with a £2,000 limit:

Balance reportedUtilisationHow lenders tend to read it
£20010%Comfortable. This is the band most files look best in.
£50025%Fine, especially if paid in full each month.
£1,00050%Starting to look stretched, even with a perfect history.
£1,50075%A warning sign to most scorecards.
£1,99099%Maxed. Reads as reliance on credit, not convenience.

The detail that catches people: agencies usually see the balance on your statement date, not the day you pay. If you spend £900 and clear it in full three days later, a 70% balance can still be reported. If you are preparing for a mortgage, make a part-payment before the statement date so the reported figure stays low.

Raising the limit also lowers utilisation — but only accept an increase you will not spend. A higher limit with the same balance is a better ratio and a worse temptation.

Searches and applications

There are two kinds, and only one is visible to other lenders:

  • Soft search. Eligibility checkers, your own report, most insurance quotes, "see your rate" tools. Not visible to lenders, no effect on your file.
  • Hard search. A full application: credit card, loan, mortgage, car finance, some mobile contracts, some current accounts with an overdraft. Visible to lenders for around a year.

Use eligibility checkers before applying. They return a probability from a soft search, so you can pick the lender most likely to say yes instead of collecting four rejections. A rejected application is not recorded as a rejection — but the hard search is, and several in a fortnight look the same as being turned down repeatedly.

If you are six months from a mortgage application, freeze new credit: no new cards, no car finance, no "buy now pay later" agreements that report, and no switching current accounts with overdraft applications unless you need to.

Fixing errors

Roughly a third of files contain something wrong. The fixes are free and mostly done in writing:

  1. Dispute the entry with the agency. Each has an online form. They must respond, usually within 28 days, and the lender has to confirm the data.
  2. Add a notice of correction. Up to 200 words explaining context — a default caused by a bank error, a period abroad, a dispute. Lenders see it when assessing your application.
  3. Contact the lender directly. For a genuinely wrong marker, the lender is the only party that can correct it at source; the agency can only pass the dispute on.
  4. Escalate. If a lender will not correct something clearly wrong, the Financial Ombudsman Service can review it, and the Information Commissioner's Office handles data accuracy complaints.

One caution: paid "credit repair" firms cannot remove accurate negative information. Nobody can. If a company promises to wipe a legitimate default, it is selling something it cannot deliver.

Myths that cost money

  • "Carrying a balance builds your score." Paying interest does not help. Paying in full each month reports the same on-time markers and costs nothing.
  • "Closing cards improves your file." Usually the opposite: less available credit means higher utilisation, and a shorter average account age.
  • "Checking my score lowers it." It does not. Self-checks are soft searches.
  • "My salary is on my credit file." Income is not in the file. Lenders ask for it separately and test affordability themselves.
  • "A default disappears when I pay it." It is marked satisfied and stays for six years. Paying still helps, because lenders treat a satisfied default better than an outstanding one.

Building from nothing

A thin file is not a bad file, but it is hard to score. If you have no credit history — moved to the UK recently, or never borrowed — start small and boring:

  • A SIM-only mobile contract in your name, paid by direct debit.
  • Council tax and utilities in your name at your address.
  • A credit builder card with a low limit, one small subscription on it, cleared by direct debit every month.
  • Rent reported through a rental reporting scheme if your letting agent offers one.

Six months of that is usually enough for a mainstream card. Twelve months of clean history is enough for most lenders to stop calling the file thin.

Credit questions

Does checking my own score lower it?

No. Looking at your own report creates a soft search that lenders cannot see. Only hard searches from credit applications are visible.

How long do defaults and CCJs stay on file?

Usually six years from the default or judgment date, whether or not you repay. Repaying marks the entry as satisfied but does not remove it early.

Should I close old credit cards?

Usually not. Closing a card reduces your available credit and raises utilisation, and it shortens your average account age. Keep it open with a small recurring payment cleared in full.

Can I get a mortgage with bad credit?

Often yes. Some lenders specialise in adverse credit and weigh the age and reason for the problem. A broker who knows which lenders use which agency can save you several rejected applications.

Do Buy Now Pay Later plans affect my file?

Some do and some do not. Several providers now report to agencies, and missed payments can be recorded. Treat it as borrowing, because a lender will.

Not advice. Credit scoring models and lender criteria change without notice, and no article can tell you whether a specific application will succeed. Check your reports directly with Experian, Equifax and TransUnion, and see MoneyHelper for free guidance.
