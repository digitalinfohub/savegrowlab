Mortgage overpayments: what each £100 actually saves

An overpayment is the cheapest guaranteed return most households will ever get — you are effectively earning your mortgage rate, tax-free, with no risk. It is also the easiest good idea to get wrong: early repayment charges, thin emergency funds and higher-interest debt all change the answer.

The arithmetic that matters

Most mortgages calculate interest daily on the outstanding balance, then collect it monthly. That single detail explains everything about overpayments:

  • Every pound repaid stops earning interest immediately. Not at the end of the month, not at the end of the fix.
  • Early money is worth more than late money. £100 overpaid in year two saves interest for the whole remaining term; the same £100 in the final year saves almost nothing.
  • Small and regular beats large and occasional for the same total, because the balance falls sooner.
  • The benefit compounds silently. A lower balance means more of next month's payment goes to capital, which lowers the balance faster still.

You do not need a formula to see the shape of it. On a long mortgage at a normal rate, overpaying a modest amount each month typically removes years from the term and a meaningful five-figure sum from the interest. Run your own numbers with the mortgage overpayment calculator rather than trusting a rule of thumb.

Early repayment charges

During a fixed or discounted deal you are usually allowed to overpay a percentage of the balance each year without charge — commonly 10%, sometimes 5% or 20%. Above that allowance, an early repayment charge applies, typically a percentage of the amount overpaid, tapering as the fix end.

Three practical points:

  1. The allowance is usually annual, not per fix. It resets on the anniversary of the deal start, not on 1 January. Check the exact date — overpaying in March and again in April can look like two years of allowance to you and one to the lender.
  2. Lump sums and monthly overpayments often share the same allowance. A generous monthly overpayment can consume it before the bonus arrives.
  3. ERCs are avoidable. If the charge is larger than the interest saved, wait until the fix ends. If it is smaller, paying the charge can still be rational — but only with the actual numbers in front of you.
Check before you pay: call the lender or read the mortgage offer document. Ask two questions — what is my annual overpayment allowance, and what charge applies above it? Get the answer in writing if you plan a large lump sum.

Term or payment?

Most lenders ask you to choose what the overpayment does. The two options produce very different outcomes:

ChoiceEffectBest for
Reduce the termMonthly payment stays the same, mortgage ends earlierMaximum interest saved; you can afford the payment already
Reduce the paymentTerm unchanged, monthly payment fallsCreating breathing room after a pay cut or a new cost

Reducing the term saves more money. Reducing the payment is a cash-flow tool, and it is reversible — you can usually switch back to the higher payment later. If you are overpaying to build a cushion rather than to finish early, a cheaper approach is often to keep the payment and hold the cash in savings, where it stays available.

Overpay or save?

The comparison is your mortgage rate against the after-tax rate on savings, adjusted for access and risk.

  • If the mortgage rate is higher than the savings rate, overpaying wins on pure arithmetic — and the "return" is tax-free.
  • If savings pay more, or the gap is small, keep the cash. Liquidity has real value: an overpayment cannot be un-paid when the boiler fails.
  • Fixed-rate savings can beat a low mortgage rate, but only if you will not need the money inside the fix.
  • Tax matters at the margin. Interest above your Personal Savings Allowance is taxed; mortgage interest saved is not.

Nothing about this is permanent. A reasonable strategy is to overpay while rates are high, and redirect the same money to savings if your fix ends at a lower rate.

When not to overpay

Overpaying is a great second move and a poor first one. Do these first:

  • Clear higher-interest debt. A credit card or car finance at a much higher rate costs more than the mortgage saves. Use the debt payoff planner to compare orders.
  • Build one month of essential costs. An overpayment is not reversible; an emergency fund is.
  • Take any employer pension match. A match is an immediate return that no overpayment can beat.
  • Check you are not near an ERC cliff edge. Waiting six weeks can save a percentage point of the balance.
  • Keep enough for the next big irregular cost — a car, a boiler, a deposit. Sinking funds before overpayments.

How to do it properly

  1. Find the annual allowance and the ERC scale in your offer document.
  2. Set a standing order for the monthly amount on the day after payday, so it leaves before it can be spent.
  3. Tell the lender whether it should reduce the term or the payment — the default is not always what you expect.
  4. Log the balance every six months. The psychological effect of seeing the curve bend is what keeps it going.
  5. Recheck when you remortgage. A new deal resets the allowance date and may change the best choice.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a limit on overpayments?

Usually 10% of the balance a year without charge during a fixed deal, though it varies by lender and product. Above that, an early repayment charge applies. Check your offer document, not a general guide.

Does overpaying reduce my monthly payment automatically?

Not always. Many lenders keep the payment the same and shorten the term unless you ask. Tell them which you want, and confirm it in writing.

Can I get an overpayment back?

Some lenders allow it if the account is up to date and the money is reclaimed within a set period, but it is not a right and it is not quick. Treat overpayments as permanent.

Is overpaying better than a savings account?

Compare your mortgage rate with the after-tax savings rate. If the mortgage rate is higher, overpaying wins; if savings pay more and you need access, save instead.

Should I overpay or invest?

Overpaying is a guaranteed return equal to your rate. Investing is not guaranteed and needs a long horizon. Most people should overpay modestly and invest regularly rather than choose one entirely.
