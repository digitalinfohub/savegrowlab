What your car actually costs you each month

Most people know their insurance and their fuel. Almost nobody adds up the MOT, the servicing, the tyres and the repairs until the year has already happened. That is why a car feels affordable in January and expensive by August. Here is the whole number.

What this guide covers

  • A worked annual running cost for a modest petrol car doing 8,000 miles.
  • The two biggest lines most budgets miss: repairs and depreciation.
  • Cost per mile, so you can compare a car with a train, a bus or a bike.
  • The specific savings that do not involve driving less.

The worked number

Assume a five-year-old petrol hatchback, 8,000 miles a year, 45 mpg, insurance for an experienced driver in a low-risk postcode, and a garage that charges a normal rate. All figures are illustrative — the structure matters more than the exact pounds.

Two things are worth noticing. First, the running costs are only about half the story — depreciation is the other half, and it is invisible because it never appears on a statement. Second, the monthly figure is what you should compare with a season ticket, not the fuel you put in on a Friday.

Fuel, done properly

The arithmetic is simple once you have the two numbers:

  1. Miles driven ÷ miles per gallon = gallons used.
  2. Gallons × 4.546 = litres.
  3. Litres × price per litre = cost.

At 8,000 miles and 45 mpg that is 177.8 gallons, 808 litres, and just over £1,170 at £1.45 a litre. Tyre pressure, roof bars, air conditioning and a heavy right foot can move that by 10–15% in either direction. A 10% improvement is about £117 a year, which is roughly the price of a service.

The fixed costs

  • Insurance. Renewal quotes are a negotiation, not a price. Check the renewal figure against two comparison sites about three weeks before it starts — that timing is when quotes are most competitive — and call your current insurer with the cheaper number.
  • VED. Rates change each April and depend on when the car was first registered and its CO2 emissions. Check the actual rate for your registration on GOV.UK rather than assuming.
  • MOT. £54.85 is the maximum a garage can charge for the test. Repairs are separate, and you can ask for the failure sheet before authorising work.
  • Servicing. The stamped book is worth real money at resale. Skipping a £280 service to save £280 can cost far more at trade-in.

Repairs and depreciation

A car is a set of consumable parts with a body around them. Brakes, discs, battery, exhaust, clutch, suspension arms and air-conditioning all fail on a schedule, and none of them announce themselves in your monthly budget. Averaging £300 a year is realistic for an older car; some years it will be £0 and some years it will be £900.

The fix is a sinking fund: £25 a month into a pot called “car”, untouched until the garage calls. It converts a crisis into an errand.

Depreciation is the largest single cost of ownership and it is highest in the first three years. A car that loses £3,000 a year is costing you £250 a month before you have bought a litre of fuel. Buying at three to five years old, and keeping the car longer, is the most reliable way to reduce it.

The cheapest car is usually the one you already own and maintain properly.

Six savings that work

  1. Renew insurance three weeks early, with two comparison quotes in hand. The same cover, a different number.
  2. Pay annually if you can. Monthly insurance is a credit agreement and usually costs more in total.
  3. Check tyre pressures monthly. Free at most supermarkets, and worth a few percent of your fuel bill.
  4. Use a trusted independent garage. Main-dealer labour rates on an eight-year-old car rarely buy you anything.
  5. Buy tyres on value, not on brand alone. Check the wet-grip and fuel-efficiency labels; the difference in stopping distance matters more than the badge.
  6. Run the “do we need two cars?” sum once a year. Add the full cost of the second car, then compare it with taxis, a bus pass and occasional hire. The answer is sometimes no.

If you are deciding between repairing and replacing, the useful threshold is usually this: if the repair costs more than the car's value, or more than about half of it and the car is high-mileage, start pricing replacements before you authorise the work.

Car costs

Car cost questions

How much does a car cost per mile?

A modest petrol car doing 8,000 miles a year costs roughly 35p a mile in running costs, or about 65p a mile once depreciation is included. Add the purchase price of the car spread over the years you keep it for the full picture.

Is leasing cheaper than buying used?

Leasing buys certainty and a new car; buying used usually costs less overall. Compare the total paid over the term — deposit, monthly payments, servicing, mileage penalties — rather than the headline monthly figure.

What is the maximum MOT fee?

£54.85 for a car. Garages can charge less, and repairs are charged separately. Ask for the failure sheet before authorising work.

Should I fix my car or replace it?

If the repair costs more than the car is worth, or more than half its value on a high-mileage car, price a replacement before agreeing to the work. A second opinion from an independent garage is usually free.

Does driving less really save much?

It saves fuel and wear, but the fixed costs — insurance, VED, MOT, depreciation — stay. That is why the decision to keep a car at all matters more than shaving 500 miles off the year.

Rates and rules change. VED, the MOT maximum fee and insurance premiums are reviewed regularly. Check GOV.UK for your vehicle's actual rate before budgeting.
