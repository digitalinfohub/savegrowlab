Home›Guides›How to cut a grocery bill without eating worse
Guide · Saving · 8 min read · Updated 10 November 2026

Food is the most responsive line in most budgets. You cannot renegotiate rent on a Tuesday evening, but you can cut £30 from a weekly shop without changing what you eat. The trick is to treat the shop as a system: plan, list, compare, repeat.

Why the weekly shop drifts

Supermarkets are designed for unplanned purchases. Essentials sit at the back, offers sit at eye level, and the basket fills while you look for milk. The average household throws away a meaningful slice of the food it buys, which means part of the bill is going straight into the bin.

None of that requires willpower to fix. It requires a list and ten minutes before you leave the house.

The four-week reset

  1. Week 1 — record. Keep every receipt. Do not change anything. At the end, add the totals. Most people underestimate their food spend by 20–30%.
  2. Week 2 — plan seven dinners. Only dinners. Breakfast and lunch are usually repetitive already. Write the ingredients, then shop from that list once.
  3. Week 3 — swap three items. Own-brand versions of cereal, pasta, tinned tomatoes, frozen vegetables and cleaning products are usually near-identical. Keep branded where you genuinely notice the difference.
  4. Week 4 — compare unit prices. Use the unit price calculator on the three products you buy most often. Bigger is not always cheaper.
Do not shop hungry, and do not shop without a list. Those two rules alone typically cut 10–15% from a bill. They are boring because they work.

The waste audit

For one week, keep a small bag for food you throw away. At the end, add up what it cost. The usual culprits: bagged salad, fresh herbs, bread, and anything bought for a recipe you did not cook. Fix them by buying frozen where texture allows, planning meals so ingredients overlap, and keeping a “use it up” shelf in the fridge.

If £8 a week goes in the bin, that is £416 a year — more than most people save by switching energy supplier.

Online versus in-store

Online shops reduce impulse buys but add substitutions and delivery fees. A workable compromise: order online for the big shop with a fixed list, then top up fresh items locally. If you use delivery passes, factor the pass into the monthly cost — and put the pass itself through the subscription audit.

Keeping the gains

A cut you cannot maintain is not a saving. Keep the meal plan, keep the list, and allow one “whatever we fancy” meal a week so the system does not feel like punishment. Send the difference to a named goal with the savings calculator — visible progress is what makes the habit survive.

Frequently asked questions

Are own-brand products actually the same?

Often they are made in the same factories to similar specifications. Try a blind swap on staples first, and keep paying for the items where you can tell the difference.

Is a warehouse membership worth it?

Only if you have storage and will use the quantities. Add the membership fee to the annual cost before deciding — the calculator in the subscription audit helps.

How much can a household realistically save?

A 10–20% reduction is common in the first month without changing meals, mostly from waste and own-brand swaps.

What about loyalty points?

Treat them as a small discount, not a reason to spend. If a loyalty scheme encourages extra trips, it is costing you money.
