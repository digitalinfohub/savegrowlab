Insurance you actually need

Insurance is not a subscription to peace of mind. It is a way to move a loss you could not survive onto a company that can. Everything else you can absorb with savings — and paying a monthly premium to cover a £200 inconvenience is the most expensive way to feel safe.

  • The one question that decides whether a policy is worth buying.
  • Which covers nearly everyone needs, and which are optional.
  • How excess, exclusions and non-disclosure decide claims.
  • The policies that quietly cost more than they return.

The test for any policy

Before you buy, answer one question: if this happened tomorrow, could I pay for it myself? If yes, you do not need insurance for it. If no, insurance is doing its job.

That single test rules out a surprising amount. A cracked phone screen is annoying, replaceable and usually cheaper than two years of premiums. A house fire is not. A broken boiler is a bad week; a serious illness that stops your income for a year is a different life.

The second test is arithmetic. Take the annual premium, multiply by the years you are likely to hold the policy, and compare it with the cost of the event and the chance of it happening. If a policy costs £180 a year to cover a £500 risk you could fund from savings, it is not insurance — it is a payment plan with admin.

Note what is absent: there is no law requiring home insurance in the UK, and no law requiring life insurance. A lender can require buildings cover as a condition of a mortgage, which is a contract term rather than legislation.

Optional covers

  • Life insurance. Needed when someone depends on your income — a partner, children, a joint mortgage. Level term cover pays a fixed amount; decreasing term falls roughly in line with a repayment mortgage and costs less.
  • Income protection. Pays a proportion of your income if illness or injury stops you working. Worth it if your employer's sick pay is short and your outgoings are committed. Check the waiting period and the definition of incapacity — "any occupation" is stricter than "own occupation".
  • Critical illness cover. Pays a lump sum on a specific diagnosis. It is narrower than people assume: the policy defines exactly which conditions and how severe they must be.
  • Pet insurance. Genuinely useful for a young dog likely to eat something it should not. Less so for an older pet with exclusions and rising premiums; at some point self-insuring with a dedicated pot is the better deal.
  • Boiler and home emergency cover. £10–£25 a month. Reasonable if your boiler is old and you have no savings; poor value if you have £2,000 accessible and could call a plumber directly.

What to skip

  • Extended warranties on appliances and electronics. Consumer rights already cover faults present at purchase, and the failure rate rarely justifies the price.
  • Payment protection insurance added to a loan or card. It has strict eligibility rules and a reputation earned the hard way.
  • Gadget and phone cover with a £100 excess on a £300 device. Compare the total premiums with the replacement cost before adding it at checkout.
  • Duplicate cover. Contents policies often include personal belongings away from home, and bank accounts sometimes bundle travel or phone cover. Check what you already have.

None of these are scams. They are simply small risks sold at a price that assumes you will not do the arithmetic.

Why claims fail

Most rejected claims come down to four things, and three are avoidable at the point of purchase:

  1. Non-disclosure. You were asked whether the property had flooded, whether you had a previous claim, whether anyone had a conviction. An inaccurate answer can void the policy even if it was accidental.
  2. Exclusions. Wear and tear, gradual damage, pests, and damage caused by poor maintenance are typically excluded. A leaking shower that rotted a floor over two years is often not a valid claim.
  3. Under-insurance. Contents sums insured must reflect replacement cost, not what you paid. Average clauses can reduce a payout proportionally.
  4. The excess. A claim below the excess costs you the excess and can still affect future premiums.

Read the exclusions page, not the marketing page. It is the only part of the document that tells you what you are not buying.

Life and income cover

Life insurance is priced on age and health, so the cheapest time to arrange it is before you need it — but "cheap" is not a reason to buy cover you do not require. Work backwards from what the money is for: clearing a mortgage, replacing income for a fixed number of years, covering childcare until school age. Write the number down, then buy cover to match it. A round £250,000 policy with no calculation behind it is usually either too much or too little.

Two structural points worth knowing:

  • Write the policy in trust. It usually costs nothing, keeps the payout outside your estate for inheritance tax, and avoids probate delay.
  • Joint policies pay once. A joint life policy typically pays on the first death only, which can leave the survivor uninsured. Two single policies can cost more but cover both lives.

For income protection, the waiting period is the dial that controls price. A policy that starts paying after 13 weeks is much cheaper than one that starts after week one — and if you have three months of expenses saved, the cheaper policy is the rational choice.

Insurance questions

Is home insurance a legal requirement?

No. Buildings insurance is normally a condition of a mortgage, but neither buildings nor contents cover is required by law in the UK.

Do I need life insurance if I have no children?

Often not. Life cover replaces income someone depends on. If nobody would struggle financially after your death, it is optional — though it is cheaper to arrange while you are young and healthy.

Why do claims get rejected?

Usually undisclosed information, an exclusion in the policy wording (wear and tear, gradual damage, maintenance), under-insurance, or a claim below the excess.

Is a higher excess better?

If you have savings, usually yes. It lowers the premium, and the purpose of insurance is the loss you could not absorb rather than a small repair you could pay for yourself.

Can I complain about an insurer?

Yes. Complain to the firm first, then the free Financial Ombudsman Service if it is not resolved. Most complaints are decided within a few months.

Not advice. Cover levels, exclusions and prices vary by insurer and change often. Compare the policy wording rather than the headline premium, and check MoneyHelper for free guidance.
