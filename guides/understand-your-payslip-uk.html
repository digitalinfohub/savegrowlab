Your payslip, decoded

Almost everyone gets a payslip, and almost nobody checks it. The lines are not secret, but they are written in payroll shorthand — so a wrong tax code or a missing pension contribution can run for a year before anyone notices. Here is what each line is for, what it should look like, and how to get it corrected.

The five lines that matter

A payslip has two columns: this period and year to date. The period column tells you what happened this month. The year-to-date column is the audit trail — it is how you prove what you were paid across the tax year, and it is the column HMRC uses when something has gone wrong.

LineWhat it isHow to sanity-check it
Gross payYour pay before any deduction, including overtime, commission, bonuses and back pay.Compare to your contract and to last month. A missing overtime line is the most common discrepancy.
Income tax (PAYE)Tax on taxable pay after your allowance and any reliefs.Should be the same or very close each month on a fixed salary. A sudden jump usually means a code change.
National InsuranceA separate contribution with its own threshold, calculated per pay period.It has a letter next to it (usually A). The letter tells you the rate category, not the amount.
PensionYour contribution, and usually your employer's, into a workplace scheme.It should appear every month once you are enrolled. If it started mid-year, check the date.
Student loanA percentage of pay above a plan-specific threshold, per pay period.The plan number matters more than the amount. A Plan 2 deduction on a Plan 5 borrower is a refund you have to chase.

Everything else — cycle-to-work, childcare vouchers, season ticket loans, union fees, salary sacrifice for a company car — is a salary sacrifice or a net deduction. Salary sacrifice reduces gross pay before tax and NI; net deductions come out after. The difference changes both your take-home and your NI record, so it is worth knowing which one you are on.

The order of operations

Deductions are not applied in the order they appear on the page. The calculation runs roughly like this:

  1. Start with gross pay.
  2. Subtract salary sacrifice arrangements (pension, cycle scheme, car). This lowers taxable pay and NIable pay.
  3. Work out income tax using your tax code and the current bands.
  4. Work out National Insurance on the reduced pay, using the current threshold and rate.
  5. Work out pension contributions under the scheme's method — net pay, relief at source or salary sacrifice.
  6. Work out student loan repayments on the relevant pay figure.
  7. Subtract net deductions such as union fees or a season ticket loan.

That is why two people on the same salary can take home different amounts: same gross, different code, different pension method, different plan. If you are comparing with a colleague, compare the structure before comparing the total.

Tax codes that cost money

Your tax code tells payroll how much of your pay is tax-free. The number is the allowance divided by ten, so 1257L means £12,570 of allowance — the standard code for one job with no untaxed income. The letter carries the rules.

CodeWhat it meansWhy it might be wrong
1257LStandard allowance, one job.Correct for most people. If you have two jobs, only one should use it.
BRAll pay taxed at basic rate, no allowance.Normal for a second job — but if your main employer has it, you are overpaying every month.
D0 / D1All pay taxed at higher or additional rate.Applied when HMRC thinks the allowance is used elsewhere.
0TNo allowance at all.Often appears when a new employer has no P45 and HMRC has not issued a code yet.
K (e.g. K475)Untaxed income or benefits are added to your taxable pay.Common after a company benefit, a P11D or an underpaid tax bill. The deduction is capped.
W1 / M1Non-cumulative: each month is taxed on its own.Common after a job change. It usually settles, but can mean a refund is delayed.
NTNo tax deducted.Rare and specific. If it appears by accident, you will owe the tax later.

The cheapest check you can run: open the HMRC app or your personal tax account, look at the current code, and compare it to the payslip. If they differ, payroll is working from an old notice. Codes also change when HMRC thinks you have untaxed income from savings, a second job, or a taxable benefit — and those estimates are sometimes wrong.

A wrong code is not free money. It is a loan from HMRC that gets collected later, usually in a lump that lands in the worst month.

The pension line

Auto-enrolment means most employees are put into a workplace pension and must contribute a minimum percentage of qualifying earnings, with the employer adding at least 3%. The total minimum is 8%, so if your line shows 5% from you, look for 3% from them. If the employer line is missing entirely, that is a question for payroll, not a rounding error.

There are three ways contributions are taken, and they are not equal:

  • Salary sacrifice. You give up part of your salary and the employer pays it in. You save income tax and National Insurance, and the employer sometimes passes on their NI saving too. It lowers your official salary, which can matter for mortgage applications and statutory pay.
  • Relief at source. You pay from net pay and the provider claims basic-rate tax back. Higher and additional rate relief has to be claimed through self-assessment — many people never do.
  • Net pay. Contributions come out before tax, so you get full relief automatically, but not NI relief. Common in larger employer schemes.

Also check the fund, not just the amount. The default fund is usually a sensible starting point, but a workplace scheme can sit in cash for years without anyone noticing. One login a year is enough to confirm the money is invested and the charges are not eating it.

Student loans

Repayments are a percentage of pay above a threshold, taken per pay period. That has two consequences people miss: a bonus month can trigger a repayment even if your annual pay is below the threshold, and a month with unpaid leave can produce no deduction at all. Neither is an error.

The plan number is the part worth checking. Plans 1, 2, 4 and 5 have different thresholds and different write-off periods, and a postgraduate loan is a separate deduction on top. If you studied in Scotland or Northern Ireland, or started after the plan changes, your employer may have the wrong one on file. The Student Loans Company can confirm it, and the fix is a form to payroll rather than a phone argument.

Five sanity checks

Run these once a quarter. Each takes under a minute and catches almost everything.

  1. Gross matches the contract. If you had a pay rise, the new gross should appear from the stated date, not the following month.
  2. Tax is stable. On a fixed salary, income tax should be within a few pounds each month. Big swings mean the code changed.
  3. The pension appears every month. Contributions should start from enrolment, and the employer amount should be visible.
  4. The year-to-date column adds up. This month's figure plus last month's year-to-date should equal this month's year-to-date.
  5. The net pay lands. Compare the net figure to the amount that actually hit your bank. If they differ, something is being paid elsewhere.

When it is wrong

Start with payroll in writing, and be specific: the line, the period, what you expected and why. Attach the contract, the P45 or the HMRC notice. Most errors are fixed in the next pay run.

If the problem is the tax code rather than the calculation, it is HMRC's to fix, not your employer's. Update your estimated income in the personal tax account, then ask payroll to apply the new code. Refunds for the current tax year usually arrive through payroll; older years are handled separately.

If an employer will not correct an underpayment, ACAS and the pay and work rights helpline are the escalation route. Keep every payslip — employees have a legal right to a written payslip on or before payday, and it is your evidence if a dispute starts.

One more thing worth checking while you are in there: your address and National Insurance number on the payslip. A single wrong digit in an NI number can leave a gap in your state pension record that takes months to untangle.

Payslip questions

Why did my pay go down when I got a pay rise?

Usually a code change, a pension contribution moving band, or a student loan repayment starting. Check the year-to-date columns before assuming the rise was calculated wrongly — a one-off correction from an earlier month is a common cause.

What does 1257L mean?

It shows the tax-free allowance being applied: the number times ten, so £12,570. It is the standard code for one job with no untaxed income or benefits.

Am I entitled to a payslip?

Yes. Employees must get a written payslip on or before payday showing gross pay, deductions and net pay. Agency workers and casual staff are included.

Do I pay student loan if I work part time?

Repayment is assessed on earnings in the pay period, not on hours. A month below the threshold has no deduction, even if your annualised pay would be above it.

Should I opt out of the pension to boost take-home pay?

Opting out returns the contribution but also loses the employer's. On a typical 8% total, walking away from 3% employer money is an immediate loss. If cash is tight, reducing other costs first is usually cheaper.

Not advice. Tax rates, thresholds, student loan plans and pension rules change, usually each April. Check GOV.UK and your HMRC personal tax account for current figures before acting.
