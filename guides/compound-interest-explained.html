Compound interest, explained with numbers you can check.

Compound interest is the single most important idea in personal finance, and the one most often wrapped in mystique. It isn't complicated: it means your earnings start earning too. What surprises people is not the mechanism but the scale — over decades, growth typically contributes more to the final total than everything you personally saved. This guide shows exactly that, with arithmetic you can verify by hand.

The one-sentence definition

Compound interest is interest calculated on last period's balance — principal plus previously accumulated interest — rather than only on the original amount. Each period's growth becomes next period's starting point, so the balance grows multiplicatively: the same percentage applied to an ever-larger number produces ever-larger cash amounts.

Simple vs compound: the £1,000 test

Leave £1,000 for five years at 5% per year and compare the two methods:

YearSimple (5% of £1,000 each year)Compound (5% of current balance)Difference
Start£1,000.00£1,000.00—
1£1,050.00£1,050.00£0.00
2£1,100.00£1,102.50£2.50
3£1,150.00£1,157.63£7.63
4£1,200.00£1,215.51£15.51
5£1,250.00£1,276.28£26.28
20£2,000.00£2,653.30£653.30

At year two the difference is pocket change. By year twenty it is a fortnight's holiday. The formula behind column three is Balance × (1 + r)n — the exponent is where all the drama lives, which is why time in the account matters far more than the rate you start chasing.

Three worked examples (3%, 5%, 7%)

Same £1,000 start, no additions, held ten years at three plausible UK rates — roughly an easy-access savings account, a competitive regular saver/cash ISA, and a long-run stock-market average before costs:

  • 3% for 10 years → £1,343.92. Growth of £343.92 on money you left completely alone.
  • 5% for 10 years → £1,628.89. Two extra percentage points added £285 — nearly as much again.
  • 7% for 10 years → £1,967.15. Almost double the original without a single additional penny contributed.

The spread between these outcomes comes almost entirely from the rate's exponent effect, not from anything clever. Now run the same three rates for 30 years: £2,427 vs £4,322 vs £7,612. The gaps stop being polite around year fifteen.

Adding monthly contributions changes everything

Compounding rewards impatience-free behaviour: steady additions plus time. Take £200/month invested at 5% compounded monthly:

By year thirty, most of the balance is money you never earned from your own labour — it arrived from the exponent. Reproduce every row with the calculator above (start £0, add £200 monthly, 5%, compound mode).

The rule of 72: doubling in your head

Divide 72 by the annual percentage to approximate how many years your money doubles at compound growth: at 3% ≈ 24 years, at 6% ≈ 12 years, at 9% ≈ 8 years. It works for the bad direction too: debt at 14.4% APR doubles in five years if untouched. The rule turns any advertised rate into a concrete, comparable intuition in about two seconds — the fastest financial literacy trick there is.

AER, gross rates and what banks actually advertise

UK savings adverts must show the AER (Annual Equivalent Rate): what you'd get in a year if interest were compounded as the account actually pays it, assuming the rate stayed fixed. Two practical consequences:

  • An account paying monthly compounds slightly faster than one paying annually at the same nominal rate — AER levels the comparison field, so compare like-for-like using AER figures.
  • AER assumes the rate doesn't change and money stays put. Variable-rate accounts can drop tomorrow; fixed-rate accounts lock today's rate but penalise early withdrawal. Neither caveat appears in the big print.

Investment platforms use different conventions again (often gross figures before fees and tax) — see the investing guide's section on costs.

The inflation edit

Compounding grows nominal pounds; prices also compound upward. If inflation averages 3% while your savings earn 4%, your real purchasing power grows about 1% a year — a twelfth of the headline rate. This is why “safe” cash quietly underperforms over long horizons and why money beyond an emergency fund may belong somewhere that can outpace prices, accepting volatility in exchange. Run your own scenario in the calculator: subtract an inflation estimate from the rate field to see the real-terms version instantly.

Five compounding mistakes (and their fixes)

  1. Waiting for a “serious” amount. £50/month started now beats £300/month started in four years across virtually every horizon tested above. Start absurdly small.
  2. Interrupting the exponent. Every raid resets the base. Keep emergency money separate precisely so the rest can be boringly untouched (fund guide).
  3. Chasing the top rate monthly. Moving savings for 0.2% costs more in disruption and missed transfers than it earns. Rate-switch twice a year at most.
  4. Ignoring fees and tax against gross returns. A 6% return minus 1% platform fee minus taxable gains is not 6%. In a cash ISA or under your Personal Savings Allowance, tax may be zero — see ISAs vs savings accounts.
  5. Assuming guaranteed compounding from investments. Savings rates are quoted per year; investment returns arrive unevenly — some years negative. The calculator's constant-rate model is a planning simplification, and honest planning keeps it that way.
Monthly vs annual compounding — does frequency matter?

More frequent compounding yields slightly more at the same nominal rate: 5% compounded monthly effectively returns 5.12% a year. Frequency differences are modest; the exponent (years) dominates by orders of magnitude.

Is compound interest the reason pensions grow?

Largely yes — workplace pensions combine regular contributions (from you, your employer and tax relief) with decades of compounded investment growth. The table in “adding monthly contributions” is essentially a pension in miniature.

Doesn't debt compound too?

Yes, identically and usually faster. Credit cards at 20–30% APR double balances in roughly 2–4 years if unpaid. Rule of 72 applies both ways — which is why clearing high-rate debt is a guaranteed “return”. Our saving guide sequences this properly.

What rate should I assume for planning?

For cash, use today's best easy-access AER minus a margin. For long-term investments, conservative planning uses 4–6% real (after inflation) over full market cycles — never the optimistic end of historic averages. Treat any single number as a scenario, not a forecast.
