Home›Guides›Sinking funds: the cure for “surprise” costs
Guide · Saving · 7 min read · Updated 10 November 2026

A car service is not an emergency. Nor is Christmas, a birthday, an annual insurance renewal or a school uniform. These costs are predictable; they only feel like emergencies because they are not monthly. A sinking fund converts them into a small monthly amount you barely notice.

Sinking fund vs emergency fund

An emergency fund is for the genuinely unexpected: job loss, a burst pipe, an urgent repair. A sinking fund is for a cost you can see coming. Keeping them separate matters — if predictable costs come out of the emergency fund, the emergency fund never grows and you conclude that you “cannot save”.

Start with three funds

Not ten. Pick the three costs that have hurt most in the last year and give each a name:

FundAnnual costMonthly set-aside
Car service, MOT and tyres£600£50
Christmas and gifts£480£40
Annual insurance and renewals£360£30
School uniforms and trips£300£25

Total: £145 a month, which sounds like a lot until it is compared with a £1,740 annual wall of “surprises”. The money was always going to be spent; the fund just decides when it leaves.

Where to keep sinking funds

Use named pots if your bank or app supports them; otherwise use a separate easy-access savings account and a simple note of what each portion is for. Do not invest sinking-fund money — the horizon is too short and the value must not fall. A cash ISA can work if the total interest would otherwise be taxed.

Practical trick: fund sinking funds in the same transfer as your main savings, split by name. One standing order, several purposes. The bank statement stays legible and nothing relies on memory.

Your first month

Pick one fund — usually car or Christmas, whichever is next on the calendar. Work out the annual cost, divide by the months remaining, and start the transfer this payday. Add a second fund next month. In three months you will have converted at least two recurring shocks into a single predictable line.

Use the goal comparison tool to see these funds alongside your other goals, and if the total feels heavy, extend the deadlines rather than abandoning the pots.

Frequently asked questions

How is a sinking fund different from saving generally?

It has a named purpose, a known amount and a date. That specificity is what stops the money being absorbed into everyday spending.

Should I keep sinking funds in the same account?

You can, but track the portions separately. Named pots make it much less likely that Christmas money gets spent in June.

What if I cannot afford all the funds?

Start with the next cost due and a one-month emergency buffer. Add funds as income or savings allow.

Do sinking funds count towards my savings rate?

They count as planned spending rather than wealth, but they still improve resilience and reduce borrowing.
