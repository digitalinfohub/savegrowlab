Your investment fees are eating the return. Here is the receipt.

A 1% annual charge does not sound like much. On a pot that grows for twenty years it is the difference between a comfortable retirement and a tight one. Fees are the one part of investing you can control with certainty, which makes them the best place to spend an hour.

What this guide covers

  • The four layers of cost in a typical DIY investment account.
  • A worked example of what 1% versus 0.2% costs over 20 years.
  • UK tax wrappers: ISA, Lifetime ISA, pension and general accounts.
  • The documents to read before you open an account, and the questions to ask.

The four layers of cost

LayerWhat it isTypical range
Platform feeThe broker's charge for holding the account. Percentage of your pot, sometimes a flat monthly fee.0.15–0.45% (DIY apps), 0.75%+ (managed services)
Fund ongoing charge (OCF)The fund manager's annual cost, deducted inside the fund.0.05–0.25% for index trackers, 0.7–1.5% for active funds
Transaction costsDealing fees, FX spread when buying overseas assets, stamp duty inside funds.Often small, occasionally brutal on frequent trading
Exit and transfer feesCharged when you leave, close or move an account.£0 at good platforms; £25–£100+ elsewhere

Add the first two together for a working “all-in” number. A cheap global tracker on a mainstream app is often 0.2–0.5% all-in. A robo-adviser or discretionary manager can be 0.8–1.5% before fund costs. Neither is automatically wrong — you are paying for something — but you should know what.

Fee drag, with numbers

Assume £10,000 to start, £200 added monthly, and 6% a year before fees for 20 years. Only the annual charge changes:

All-in annual chargePot after 20 yearsLost to fees vs 0.2%
0.20%£122,060—
0.45%£117,903£4,157
1.00%£109,333£12,727
1.20%£106,402£15,658

£15,658 is not a rounding error. It is a car, a house deposit top-up, or two years of energy bills — paid to a provider for the privilege of holding the same underlying companies. And the gap widens with a larger pot or a longer horizon, because the fee compounds against you exactly as returns compound for you.

Real return, not headline return

If your fund returns 6% and inflation runs at 3%, your real return is roughly 3% before fees. Take 1% of charges and it is 2%. You have just given away a third of your purchasing-power gain. This is why “my fund made 6%” is not a useful sentence on its own — the number that matters is what is left after inflation and costs.

Also be sceptical of past performance charts. They usually show gross returns, start at a favourable date, and ignore the platform fee. A fund that beat its index for five years may simply have taken more risk. The compound interest calculator lets you test a pessimistic rate rather than the flattering one — that is the column to plan around.

Wrappers and allowances

WrapperCurrent headline allowanceTax on growthAccess
Stocks & Shares ISA£20,000 a yearNoneAny time
Lifetime ISA£4,000 a year (counts towards the ISA limit)None, plus 25% bonusFirst home or age 60; 25% charge otherwise
Workplace / personal pension£60,000 annual allowance, tapered for high earnersNone inside; taxed as income on withdrawalAge 55–57+, rising
General investment accountNo limitCapital gains and dividends can be taxedAny time

Outside a wrapper, the annual Capital Gains Tax exempt amount is currently £3,000 and the dividend allowance £500. The Personal Savings Allowance depends on your tax band. These figures move with budgets — check gov.uk before relying on them.

One practical rule: use the ISA allowance before a general account. It costs nothing to shelter the money and you cannot recover an unused year. For goals before retirement, an ISA usually beats a pension on flexibility. For retirement, a pension's tax relief and employer match usually beat the ISA. The pension guide covers that trade-off properly.

Fee check

Cheap is not always better

A 0.1% platform with a broken app, no phone support and a 1% FX charge may cost more than a 0.3% platform that does the boring things well. The questions that matter are: does it hold the funds you want, is the fee structure transparent, does it protect your assets under FSCS rules, and can you leave without a penalty? Pay for service if you value it. Do not pay 1% for a default fund you could buy for 0.2%.

The 80/20 of investing costs: choose one low-cost global tracker, hold it in the right wrapper, and stop trading. Most of the fee damage comes from layering an expensive platform on an expensive fund and paying dealing charges to tinker.

Fees and tax questions

Is a 1% fee really that bad?

On its own it is survivable. Combined with inflation and a mediocre fund it can consume a large share of your real return. The example above shows £15,658 lost over 20 years versus a 0.2% charge on the same investments.

What is a good all-in fee?

For a simple global tracker on a DIY platform, roughly 0.2–0.5% all-in is competitive. Managed services charge more because they provide more, but you should be able to name what you are buying.

Do I pay tax inside an ISA?

No income tax or capital gains tax on investments held in an ISA. Stamp duty and some withholding taxes can still apply inside funds, which is one reason the OCF is not the whole story.

How much can I put in an ISA?

The current annual ISA allowance is £20,000 across all ISA types, with a separate £4,000 limit for a Lifetime ISA. Unused allowance does not roll over.

Should I move an old pension to a cheaper platform?

Sometimes, but check exit fees, guaranteed annuity rates, protected pension ages and any employer contributions you would lose. A transfer is not automatically an improvement.
