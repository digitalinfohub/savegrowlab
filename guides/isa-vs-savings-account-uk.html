ISAs vs savings accounts: where should your money sit?

“Should I move my savings into an ISA?” is one of the most common money questions in Britain, and the honest answer is “it depends on three numbers you can look up tonight.” This guide untangles the confusion — because an ISA isn't a rival product to a savings account; it's a wrapper that can contain one — then walks through tax allowances, protection limits and access rules, and finishes with a decision table mapping each pile of your money to its best home.

Key idea first: there are two different things called “ISA” that matter here — a Cash ISA (a tax-free savings account) and a Stocks & Shares ISA (a tax-free investment wrapper). Comparing them against an ordinary savings account is really asking: how much does the tax wrapper change my outcome?

Wrapper vs product: clearing the confusion

A current account, an easy-access savings account and a fixed bond are products — they define what you get: access terms and interest. An ISA is a wrapper — it defines what HMRC sees: nothing taxable inside it. You can wrap a savings product in an ISA (“cash ISA”) or wrap investments in one (“Stocks & Shares ISA”). Once that distinction lands, the real question becomes narrow and answerable: for this specific pile of money, does the wrapper earn me more than it costs me in rate or flexibility?

The Personal Savings Allowance: when tax already doesn't apply

Since 2016, most UK savers pay no tax on savings interest anyway, via the Personal Savings Allowance (PSA):

  • Basic-rate taxpayers (20%): up to £1,000 of interest per year tax-free.
  • Higher-rate (40%): up to £500 tax-free.
  • Additional-rate (45%): nil — all interest taxed.

At today's typical rates around 4–5%, a basic-rate taxpayer needs roughly £20,000–£25,000 in savings before interest breaches £1,000. Below that line, a cash ISA shelters interest you were never going to pay tax on — which is why most ordinary savers don't need one. Above it, or if you're a higher/additional-rate earner, the wrapper starts pulling real weight. (Also note: savings income counts toward your total income and can pull borderline earners into higher bands.) Exact figures change with fiscal events — verify at gov.uk.

The £20,000 ISA allowance mechanics

Each tax year (6 April–5 April) you may subscribe up to £20,000 across ISAs — split however you like between cash and Stocks & Shares versions (e.g. £5k + £15k). Three rules trip people up:

  1. Subscriptions, not balances. The limit is what you put in this year, not what sits in there. A £100k inherited ISA balance from years past is fine.
  2. No carry-forward. Unused allowance evaporates on 5 April. If you want to use it, transfer money in before the deadline — moving between same-year accounts after the new year started doesn't restore it.
  3. Withdrawals can complicate re-entry mid-year. With most flexible ISAs, taking money out and putting it back in the same tax year can count as a fresh subscription. Plan transfers around tax-year boundaries.

Rate comparison: why cash ISAs often lose on headline

Because so many savers don't need the wrapper (see PSA above), providers price regular easy-access accounts more aggressively than cash ISAs — the ISA label alone rarely wins a rate comparison. The pattern to expect: best fixed bonds ≥ best regular easy-access ≥ best cash ISA ≥ big-bank easy-access. So for sub-allowance savers, an ordinary account usually delivers strictly more pounds. The wrapper pays off when (a) interest exceeds your PSA, (b) you're higher/additional rate, or (c) you value simplicity and consolidate everything under one roof regardless of a few basis points.

FSCS protection — identical either way

A common misconception is that ISAs are “safer.” They aren't, and they aren't riskier: protection comes from the provider's regulatory status, not the wrapper. Eligible deposits — ISA or not — are protected up to £85,000 per person, per licensed institution by the Financial Services Compensation Scheme. Two practical consequences: large balances spread across separate banking groups stay fully covered (watch for brands sharing one licence despite different names), and Stocks & Shares ISA holdings are not deposits — they carry investment risk instead of FSCS cover, addressed in our investing guide.

Worked example: £15,000 saved at 4.5%

Single depositor earning £38,000 (basic rate), £15,000 in a 4.5% AER easy-access account. Annual interest ≈ £675 — comfortably below the £1,000 PSA, so tax bill: zero. Moving it into a cash ISA paying 3.9% would cost about £90/year for a shelter worth nothing. Now raise the stakes: add £20,000 inheritance → £35,000 generating ≈ £1,575, breaching the PSA by £575; £115 tax due at 20%. Shifting enough into a 3.9% cash ISA to keep taxable interest under £1,000 now saves real money — the wrapper flipped from pointless to useful somewhere around the £22k mark. Four traps to avoid

  • Confusing gross rate with net outcome. A 5% account that pushes you over the PSA isn't automatically better than 4.5% inside an ISA. Compare after-tax whenever balances approach the threshold.
  • Locking emergency money “for the better ISA rate.” Fixed cash ISAs combine both downsides if life interrupts: penalty plus lost wrapper headroom.
  • Missing the 5 April deadline. Use-it-or-lose-it allowance burns quietly each spring. Calendar reminder: late March.
  • Assuming all “instant access” is instant. Some notice accounts take 30–90 days; check withdrawal terms before trusting them with anything time-sensitive.
Can I open both a Cash ISA and a Stocks & Shares ISA this year?

Yes — share the single £20,000 allowance between them any way you like. Splitting subscriptions across providers within the same type has historically had timing rules; confirm current-year guidance with HMRC before arranging multiple transfers.

Do ISAs affect means-tested benefits or Universal Credit?

Savings capital generally counts for benefit purposes whether wrapped or not — the wrapper hides tax, not assets. Check current thresholds on gov.uk or with Citizens Advice if relevant to you.

What happens to an ISA when someone dies?

A surviving spouse/civil partner can receive an additional “ASA” (Additional Subscription Amount) mirroring the deceased's ISA balances, preserving tax-free status. It's paperwork-heavy; the executor handles it via the provider.

Junior ISAs — worth starting?

£9,000/year (current limit) sheltered until 18 is one of the strongest tax gifts available for children; long horizons suit equity wrappers. Locking away the money is the trade-off — decide consciously.
