Bank switching: the £150 that most people leave on the table

Switching current accounts is one of the few genuinely easy wins in personal finance. The paperwork is done for you, the transfer is guaranteed by a formal service, and the banks pay you to arrive. It only goes wrong when people switch the wrong account, or misunderstand the conditions attached to the bonus.

  • What the Current Account Switch Service actually does, and what it does not.
  • The four conditions almost every switching bonus hides in the small print.
  • Which account to switch — and which never to touch.

  • The tax and credit-file consequences people forget.

What the switch service does

The Current Account Switch Service (CASS) is a free, industry-run guarantee. When you switch through it, the new bank does the work and the old account closes on a date you agree, usually within seven working days.

  • Direct debits and standing orders move automatically. You do not need to contact the energy company, the council or the gym.
  • Incoming payments are redirected. Salary, benefits and transfers to your old details are forwarded automatically — for three years, not forever.
  • You are protected from charges. If something goes wrong in the switch and you are charged by either bank, the switch guarantee covers the cost.
  • The old account closes. That is the point, and also the risk: anything attached to it that is not a direct debit, standing order or incoming payment does not move.
What does not move: saved payees, card subscriptions billed to the old card, linked savings accounts, PayPal and Apple Pay links, and anything paid by card rather than direct debit. Re-check those in the first week.

The conditions in the small print

A switching offer is a contract: the bank pays you if you do a set of things within a set window. Advertising usually names one condition; the terms contain four. Read them before you start, because the clock often begins at application, not at switch.

ConditionWhat it usually meansHow people fail it
Full switch through CASSAt least two direct debits or standing orders must move with the accountSwitching an empty account with no direct debits
Minimum monthly pay-inOften £500–£1,500 of credits per monthMoving a small amount in and out on the same day, which some banks exclude
Stay for a minimum periodUsually three to twelve monthsSwitching again before the retention date and losing the bonus
New customers onlyNo account with that banking group in the last few yearsForgetting a closed account or a joint account held with the same group

Some offers pay in two stages — part on switching, part after a few months of qualifying activity. Those are still worth taking, but diarise the second date or it quietly expires.

Which account to switch

Switch a secondary account, not the one your life runs through. The switch is reliable, but salary delays, a missed direct debit or a card that stops working for a few days are avoidable risks when you have a spare account.

A workable setup for most people:

  1. Keep the main account where salary lands and bills are paid. Leave it alone.
  2. Open a cheap second account at a different banking group, and move two small direct debits to it — a phone contract and a streaming subscription are ideal.
  3. Switch that second account to the bank paying the bonus. The direct debits move with it.
  4. Set up the pay-in as a standing order from your main account, and let it sit there if the account pays interest.

Never switch a joint account without both account holders applying, and never switch an account with an arranged overdraft you are using — the new bank can refuse the transfer, or call in the overdraft.

Switching more than once

Doing two or three switches a year is normal and legal. The constraint is the retention period and the "new customer" rule, not a limit on how often you can switch. A simple cycle:

  • Complete switch one, collect the bonus, keep the required pay-in and direct debits running.
  • When the retention date passes, switch that same account on to the next offer. The direct debits travel with it, so you meet the next bank's condition automatically.
  • Keep a note of each bank's exclusion window. Most are measured in years, and a joint account with the same group usually counts as a relationship.

Do not chase an offer that requires you to pay a monthly fee larger than the bonus unless the account's other benefits genuinely cover it. A £150 bonus against a £15 monthly fee is gone in ten months.

Credit files, overdrafts and tax

A current account application usually creates a hard search, which appears on your file and can affect a mortgage application in the following months. If you are applying for a mortgage, a remortgage or a large loan, postpone the switching game until it completes — the bonus is not worth a declined application.

Interest earned on the balance is taxable like any other savings interest, though the Personal Savings Allowance means most people pay nothing. If you are a higher-rate taxpayer with substantial savings, keep the total in mind rather than assuming bank interest is tax-free.

Closing an old account can also remove an overdraft facility you were relying on. That is not a credit-file disaster, but it can leave a gap if an unexpected bill lands in the same week.

Traps that cost the bonus

  • Switching before reading the retention date. The most common reason a bonus never arrives.
  • Card subscriptions. A gym or streaming service billed to the old debit card will fail silently after the switch. Update them in week one.
  • Linked savers. Some regular saver accounts are attached to a specific current account and close or lose their rate when it closes.
  • Employer payroll cut-offs. Redirects cover salary, but payroll teams sometimes refuse redirected payments. Tell payroll in advance.
  • Bonus clawback. A few banks reclaim the bonus if you close within the retention window. Check before switching again.

Frequently asked questions

Does switching hurt my credit score?

Usually only mildly and briefly. The application creates a hard search, and closing an old account can reduce your available credit. Neither is damaging on its own; both matter if you are about to apply for a mortgage.

How long does a switch take?

Seven working days is the standard guarantee, and you choose the completion date. Keep both accounts funded for a few days either side in case a payment arrives at the wrong moment.

Can I keep my old account open?

Not with a full CASS switch — the old account closes. If you want to keep it, do a partial switch where the bank offers one, but a partial switch usually does not qualify for the bonus.

What if the bonus does not arrive?

Complain to the bank in writing, quoting the offer terms and your qualifying dates. If it is not resolved in eight weeks, the Financial Ombudsman Service can look at it free of charge.

Is the bonus taxable?

Cash incentives for switching are generally treated as a discount or a cashback rather than income, and are not taxed as earnings. Interest on the balance is taxable in the normal way, subject to your Personal Savings Allowance.
