Cut the energy bill without spending a penny first

Energy is one of the few bills you can reduce by changing behaviour today and never notice the difference. The order matters: free habits first, cheap fixes second, and only then anything that costs real money. Here is the whole ladder, with the parts most people skip.

What this guide covers

  • How a bill is built: standing charge, unit rate, and usage.
  • The three free settings that move the number most — thermostat, flow temperature and timers.
  • Cheap physical fixes under £50 that keep working for years.
  • When switching tariff or supplier is worth the effort, and when it is not.

What you are actually paying for

Every bill has three parts, and only one of them responds to behaviour:

PartWhat it isCan you change it?
Standing chargeA fixed daily amount for the meter, network and supply — commonly 40–60p a day for electricityOnly by changing tariff or supplier
Unit ratePrice per kWh, set by your tariffBy switching tariff, not by using less
UsageHow many kWh you actually consumeYes — this is the lever

A standing charge of 50p a day is about £182 a year before you switch anything on. That is why a low-usage household can still feel the bill is unfair: a large slice is fixed. It also means a tariff with a lower unit rate and a higher standing charge only wins if you use enough energy to justify it.

The price cap is not a cap on your bill — it caps the unit rate and standing charge a supplier can charge on a default tariff. Use more, pay more. The cap is reviewed quarterly, which is why the number in the news keeps moving.

The free changes, ranked

1. Turn the thermostat down by one degree

Roughly a tenth of heating energy goes with each degree. On a £1,200 annual gas bill, one degree is around £120 — and most people cannot tell the difference after three days. Set it at 18–19°C when you are in, lower at night, and off when the house is empty. Do not turn it up to heat the house faster; it heats at the same rate and overshoots.

2. Lower the boiler flow temperature

If you have a condensing combi, the flow temperature is probably set at 70–80°C from installation. Dropping it to around 60°C for heating — while leaving hot water hot — lets the boiler condense for more of the time, which is where the efficiency comes from. On many systems this is a two-button change. If radiators stop getting warm on a cold day, nudge it back up by five degrees.

3. Use the timer instead of the “always on” habit

Heat the house for the hours you are in it: roughly 30 minutes before you wake and 30 minutes before you return, off an hour before bed. A well-insulated home loses heat whatever you do, so heating an empty house is a straight loss.

The cheapest unit of energy is the one you did not use, and the second cheapest is the one you used at a lower flow temperature.

Fixes under £50

  • Draught-proofing. Letterbox flap, chimney balloon, door brush, window seals. A cold draught makes people turn the thermostat up, so stopping it saves twice.
  • Bleed the radiators. Cold at the top means air. A radiator that never gets hot is a room you are heating with the thermostat instead.
  • Thermostatic radiator valves. Set bedrooms lower than living rooms. Heating every room to the same temperature is the most common waste in a house.
  • Radiator reflector panels. A few pounds each for external walls, and they push heat back into the room instead of into the brickwork.
  • Cylinder jacket. If you have a hot water tank, an 80mm jacket pays for itself in months. Keep the thermostat at 60°C for safety.
  • Curtains and furniture. Do not put a sofa in front of a radiator. Close curtains at dusk; open them on a sunny south-facing side in winter.

Loft insulation is the big one if it is thin or missing — 270mm is the usual recommendation. It costs more than £50, but it is the highest-return physical change in most UK homes, and there are sometimes supplier or local-authority schemes.

Appliances and hot water

  • The tumble dryer is the most expensive appliance per hour. A heated airer or an extra spin cycle costs a fraction. If you use the dryer, fill it.
  • Wash at 30°C and use full loads. Most of the energy in a wash goes on heating the water.
  • Showers beat baths, but a long shower beats a short one. Four minutes is the target; a timer is free.
  • Kettles: boil only what you need. It is a tiny saving per cup and a real one per year in a household of four.
  • Fridges and freezers: keep them away from the oven and out of direct sun, and defrost the freezer if it ices up. A full freezer runs better than an empty one.
  • Standby: TVs, consoles and set-top boxes on standby are small individually. Add up the house and it is worth a switched extension lead.

Economy 7 and other time-of-use tariffs only win if you can shift load into the cheap window — storage heaters, an electric vehicle, a heat pump with a thermal store. On a standard gas-heated home, they usually lose.

Tariffs and switching

Switching is worth checking twice a year, not twice a week. The useful comparison is the annual estimate for your actual usage, not the headline unit rate, and not the monthly direct debit — suppliers set that number and it drifts.

  • Give a meter reading on the day you switch so the old supplier cannot estimate you into a credit balance you then chase for months.
  • Check the exit fee before fixing. A £75 exit fee on a tariff that saves £60 is a loss.
  • In spring, check whether your direct debit has built a large credit. Ask for it back or ask for the payment to be recalculated.
  • If you are on a prepayment meter, compare standing charges specifically — they vary more than people expect.

Ignore any approach by phone, text or door claiming to be from your supplier or the government offering a discount. Energy scams are common and they target exactly the anxiety this bill creates.

Energy questions

Is it cheaper to leave the heating on all day?

No. A well-insulated home loses heat either way, so heating an empty house costs more than reheating it. Use a timer for the hours you are actually there.

What is a standing charge?

A fixed daily amount covering the meter, network and supply, charged whether you use energy or not — commonly 40–60p a day for electricity. It is why a low-usage bill still has a floor.

Does a smart meter save money?

Not on its own. It makes usage visible, which tends to reduce it, but the saving comes from the habit change or the tariff. In-home displays often end up in a drawer.

Should I fix my tariff?

Fix if certainty matters more than the possibility of a cheaper variable rate, and only if the unit rate is competitive for your usage. The price cap moves quarterly, so compare the annual estimate instead of guessing.

Will lowering the flow temperature make the house cold?

It usually takes longer to heat, not colder at steady state, because the radiators run for longer at a lower temperature. If a cold snap leaves rooms short, raise it five degrees rather than abandoning the change.

Check before you act on the building. If you rent, ask the landlord before changing boiler settings or adding insulation. If you are on certain benefits or a low income, the Warm Home Discount and supplier hardship funds may apply — ask your supplier directly; they are not always advertised.
