The employer match is free money. Are you collecting all of it?

Most UK employees are auto-enrolled into a workplace pension and most of them contribute the minimum. That is a reasonable start and a poor finish. The gap between the statutory minimum and what your employer will actually add can be worth tens of thousands of pounds over a career — and it is the only guaranteed return in personal finance.

What this guide covers

  • What auto-enrolment guarantees, and what your employer may offer above it.
  • How tax relief turns £80 into £100 (or more) before any investment return.
  • A worked salary example showing the real cost of raising your contribution.
  • Salary sacrifice, old pots, fees, and when a pension is the wrong home for your money.

What the law guarantees

Automatic enrolment means your employer must put you into a pension scheme if you are aged between 22 and State Pension age and earn above the earnings trigger. The statutory minimum is 8% of qualifying earnings, of which at least 3% must come from your employer. Qualifying earnings are a band, not your whole salary — currently £6,240 to £50,270, though the figures are reviewed and can change.

You5% of qualifying earnings5–8%
Employer3%5–10%, often matched to your contribution
Government (tax relief)20% on your contribution for basic-rate taxpayersSame, plus more for higher earners via self-assessment
Total into the pot8% + relief10–18% + relief

The important word is minimum. Many employers will match contributions above 5%, sometimes up to 8% or 10%. That matching policy is usually buried in your contract or intranet, not in the enrolment letter. Find it. It is the highest-yield document you will read this year.

The maths of the match

Take a £32,000 salary. Qualifying earnings are £25,760 (£32,000 minus the £6,240 lower limit). At the statutory minimum, 8% of that is £2,060.80 a year — about £171.73 a month, split £1,288 from you and £772.80 from your employer. Your employer is already handing you £772.80 a year for doing nothing.

Now suppose your employer matches up to 8% and you are contributing 5%. Raising your contribution from 5% to 8% means £772.80 more from you — but basic-rate tax relief means the real cost is roughly £618 of take-home pay. In exchange, your pot receives your extra £772.80 plus another £772.80 of employer money. You have turned about £618 of spending into £1,545 of pension. There is no savings account, investment or side hustle that reliably does that.

Every year you leave an employer match unclaimed, you decline a pay rise that is already budgeted for.

Over 30 years at an illustrative 5% annual growth, the statutory-minimum contribution of £171.73 a month grows to roughly £143,000. At 11% total (8% you, 3% employer) the monthly figure is £236.13 and the pot is nearer £196,000. The difference is not magic; it is the extra contribution plus compounding on money that was never yours to spend. These are projections, not promises — investments can fall.

Tax relief, three ways

How the government top-up arrives depends on your scheme:

  • Relief at source. You pay £80, the provider claims £20 from HMRC and invests £100. Higher and additional rate taxpayers claim the extra relief through self-assessment.
  • Net pay arrangement. Your contribution comes out before income tax is calculated, so the relief is automatic. Lower earners can lose out here if they pay no income tax at all.
  • Salary sacrifice. You agree to a lower salary in exchange for a higher employer pension contribution. Income tax and usually National Insurance are avoided on the sacrificed amount — see below.

None of this changes the investment inside the pension. It just means more money arrives there. A workplace pension is a wrapper plus a default fund; the wrapper is generous, the default fund is often mediocre but rarely terrible.

Salary sacrifice, briefly

Salary sacrifice reduces your contractual salary and pays the difference into your pension. Because National Insurance is charged on salary, both you and your employer can save NI — and some employers pass their saving on. The trade-offs are real: a lower salary can affect mortgage affordability, statutory maternity pay, and some benefits. It is usually excellent for higher earners and worth asking about, not worth doing blindly. Ask payroll for the numbers in writing.

When a pension is not the answer

Pensions lock money away until your late fifties (the age is rising). If your emergency fund is empty or you are carrying a credit card at 22%, those come first — the autopilot ranks them for you. Beyond the employer match, the order is usually:

  1. One month of essential costs in cash.
  2. Any debt above roughly 10% APR.
  3. The full employer match.
  4. Three to six months of essentials.
  5. A Stocks & Shares ISA or Lifetime ISA for goals before retirement.
  6. More pension, especially if you are a higher-rate taxpayer.

If you are self-employed there is no employer match, but tax relief still applies and you can open a personal or stakeholder pension. The same logic holds: the tax wrapper is the edge, the fund is the engine.

A note on costs and claims

Workplace schemes vary enormously. A 0.3% charge versus 1% on a £60,000 pot is £420 a year, every year, compounding against you. Ask for the charges breakdown in writing. And be sceptical of anyone who contacts you about “reviewing your pension” or transferring it into something exotic — pension scams are common and the losses are usually permanent.

Not advice. This is general education. Contribution limits, qualifying earnings, tax bands and access ages change. Confirm your own position on gov.uk or with a regulated adviser before making a decision.

Pension questions

Do I have to contribute 5%?

The statutory minimum is 8% of qualifying earnings in total, with at least 3% from your employer. That leaves 5% from you in a typical scheme. Your employer may require more, or match more — check your contract.

What if I cannot afford the extra contribution?

Raise it by one percentage point at your next pay review rather than jumping to the maximum. A 1% increase is usually a few pounds a month and is much easier to keep.

Can I take my pension early?

Not normally before 55, rising to 57 and later. Early access is only in limited circumstances such as serious ill health. Treat the money as unavailable.

What happens to my pension if I change jobs?

It stays yours. You can leave it, transfer it to a new scheme, or combine old pots. Check exit fees and whether you lose any valuable guarantees before transferring.

Is a workplace pension a good investment?

The wrapper is excellent because of tax relief and employer contributions. The default fund is usually diversified but can be conservative or expensive. Check the fund charge and whether a different option in the scheme suits your horizon.
