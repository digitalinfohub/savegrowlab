The house deposit: stop guessing the number

Most people saving for a home are working towards a figure someone else mentioned once. The number is not mysterious — it is a percentage of the price, plus fees nobody warns you about, plus a timeline your income can actually support. Here is the whole sum, in order.

What this guide covers

  • What a 5%, 10% and 15% deposit actually costs at today's prices.
  • The £2,000–£4,000 of buying costs that sit outside the deposit.
  • How the Lifetime ISA 25% bonus works, and when it bites you.
  • A savings timeline you can check against your own monthly amount.

The number, in one table

A deposit is a percentage of the purchase price, so the figure moves with the market. Lenders typically want at least 5%, prefer 10%, and give better rates from 15–20% upwards. On a £250,000 first home — roughly the UK average — the difference between 5% and 10% is £12,500 of cash.

Do not save to the exact percentage. Save to the percentage plus a cushion, because the home you end up buying is usually a little more expensive than the one you started viewing, and the survey will find something.

Costs that are not the deposit

This is where budgets fail. The deposit gets all the attention and the transaction costs arrive in the final six weeks, all at once:

  • Conveyancing and searches. Roughly £1,000–£2,000 for a freehold purchase, more for leasehold.
  • Survey. £400–£700 depending on the level of inspection.
  • Mortgage fees. Arrangement fees range from nothing to about £1,000; a valuation fee is sometimes included, sometimes not.
  • Moving. £300–£800 for a local move, considerably more with a long distance and a lot of furniture.
  • The boring first shop. A new home needs bins, a bed, a washing machine, a hoover. Assume four figures and be pleasantly surprised.

A practical rule: add £3,000 to whatever deposit you are targeting, then check that total against the timeline. If £3,000 feels impossible on top, the honest answer is that you are not saving for a deposit yet — you are saving for a deposit and a move, and both need funding.

The Lifetime ISA, honestly

A Lifetime ISA is the best-value wrapper for most first-time buyers, and also the one with the sharpest edge. The rules that matter:

  • You can open one between 18 and 39, and pay in until you are 50.
  • The government adds 25% on up to £4,000 of contributions a year — a maximum £1,000 bonus annually.
  • The property must cost £450,000 or less and you must be a first-time buyer.
  • The account must have been open for at least 12 months before you use it for a home.

Save £4,000 a year into a LISA and the bonus is £1,000 — an instant 25% return before any interest. Over four years that is £4,000 of government money towards the deposit, which is more than most people save in a year.

The edge: withdraw for anything other than a qualifying home, retirement or terminal illness and you lose the bonus and about 6.25% of your own money. £4,000 in becomes £5,000 with the bonus, and a 25% charge on that leaves £3,750. Do not put money in a LISA that you might need for a car, a wedding or a bad month. Keep the emergency fund separate in an ordinary account.

The bonus is free money with a lock on it. Treat the LISA as a deposit-only pot and it is the best deal available to a first-time buyer.

How long it really takes

Divide the target by what you can genuinely move each month. At £600 a month, £30,000 takes 50 months — just over four years. At £800 it takes 38 months. At £1,000 it takes 30. These are the numbers that decide whether the plan is real, not the aspiration.

Two levers shorten that timeline without changing your lifestyle: a higher income and a cheaper target. The income guide covers the first; the second is a decision about area, size and condition rather than a sacrifice you make every week.

Interest is almost irrelevant here. A deposit sits in cash, cash rates roughly track inflation, and the point of the pot is certainty rather than growth. Do not let a 0.4% difference in savings rates delay a decision by a year.

Where to keep the money

  • LISA first, up to £4,000 a year, if you meet the criteria and the property cap is realistic for your area.
  • The rest in an easy-access or notice account in your own name, ideally one that is not attached to your debit card.
  • Not in investments if the purchase is within two or three years. A 20% fall in the month before you buy is not a risk you can wait out.
  • Not in a joint account unless you are buying together and both names are on the mortgage. Ownership of the deposit is worth documenting if someone else is contributing.

If family are gifting part of the deposit, ask for a short signed letter confirming it is a gift, not a loan. Lenders ask for this and it delays purchases when it is missing.

Deposit questions

Is a 5% deposit enough?

It can be — 95% loan-to-value mortgages exist — but the rate is usually higher and affordability checks are stricter. Moving from 5% to 10% often lowers the rate enough to be worth waiting for, and it reduces what you owe if prices fall.

Can I use a Lifetime ISA for a house?

Yes, if you are a first-time buyer, the property costs £450,000 or less and the account has been open at least 12 months. You get a 25% bonus on up to £4,000 of contributions each tax year.

Should I invest my deposit?

Usually not within two to three years of buying. Investments can fall 20% in a bad year, and a deposit has a date attached. Cash is boring and appropriate here.

Do I need a deposit if I use a guarantor?

Some schemes and family-assisted mortgages reduce the deposit requirement, but they are not free — a guarantor carries real risk. Compare the total cost, not just the cash needed on day one.

What about Shared Ownership?

You buy a share of the home and pay rent on the rest. It lowers the deposit needed, but you need to model the rent, service charge and staircasing costs. It suits some buyers and traps others.

Not advice. Deposit rules, scheme criteria and the Lifetime ISA cap can change. Check GOV.UK and MoneyHelper, and speak to a regulated mortgage adviser before committing.
