Budgeting when your pay is never the same twice

Every budgeting method assumes a number that arrives on the same date each month. Zero-hours work, agency shifts, commission, freelance invoices and seasonal trade do not work that way. The fix is not a better spreadsheet — it is a different shape of budget, built around your lowest month rather than your best one.

What this guide covers

  • How to find a baseline income you can actually plan on.
  • The two-account smoothing system that ends the feast-and-famine cycle.
  • What to pay first when the month is short.
  • How to set aside tax and pension when nobody does it for you.
  • Why the emergency fund matters more, not less, on variable pay.

Finding your baseline

A budget based on your average income is a budget that fails about half the time, because half your months are below average. Build it on the lowest normal month instead.

  1. List the last twelve months of net pay — the amount that actually landed, after tax and any deductions.
  2. Ignore the single worst month if it was a one-off (illness, a gap between contracts) and ignore the best if it was a bonus. You are looking for the floor, not the outliers.
  3. Take the lowest of the remaining months. That is your baseline.
  4. Build the essential part of your budget to fit inside the baseline, not the average.

If the baseline does not cover rent, food and bills, the budget is not the problem — the income is. That is the moment to use the income side and to check what help you are entitled to, rather than shaving £8 off a food shop and calling it solved.

The smoothing account

The core mechanic is simple: money arrives in lumps and leaves in a steady stream. Put a second account between the two.

  • Account one receives every payment — wages, invoices, commission, tips.
  • Account two pays the fixed bills by direct debit. On the same date each month, move a fixed amount from account one into account two. That amount is your baseline budget.
  • What is left in account one is genuinely spare, and it can be split between a buffer, debt and a life that is not entirely beans.

The first three months are the hard part, because the smoothing account starts empty and a low month arrives before a buffer exists. Two ways through it: hold back one good month deliberately (treat it as a cost of the system, not a loss), or start with a small overdraft-free float from savings and repay it from the next strong month.

Do not smooth by borrowing. Paying bills on a credit card in the short month and clearing it in the strong month works exactly once, then a short month arrives with an existing balance and the whole plan collapses into interest.

The priority order

When a month is short, the order you pay things in decides how bad the damage gets. Priority debts carry consequences beyond a late fee: losing a home, a car, heat or a licence.

  1. Rent or mortgage. Housing arrears escalate faster than any consumer debt.
  2. Council Tax. A priority debt with a fast court route.
  3. Energy and water. Both have protection rules, and both get more expensive once arrears build.
  4. Insurance and car costs if you need the vehicle to earn.
  5. Minimum payments on credit agreements, to avoid default markers.
  6. Everything else — subscriptions, non-urgent repairs, savings.

If you cannot cover 1–3, contact each creditor before the due date and ask for a payment arrangement. Councils, energy suppliers and mortgage lenders all have documented forbearance processes; quiet non-payment does not trigger them, asking does.

Percentages, not amounts

With variable income, fixed pound targets fight you every month. Percentages scale automatically. A workable split for each payment as it arrives:

ShareGoes toWhy
60–65%Essentials — housing, bills, food, transportAlready sized to the baseline month, so this share is the one that must hold.
10%Smoothing bufferFills the gap in a short month. Once it holds one month of essentials, redirect this share.
10%Debt overpaymentsOnly after minimums are covered. Stop when the rate is low and the buffer is thin.
10%Irregular costs — car service, dentist, ChristmasSinking funds stop these becoming emergencies.
5–10%Long-term saving and investingSmall and automatic beats large and occasional.

The percentages are a starting point, not a rule. The important part is that the same split applies to a £900 month and a £2,400 month, so a good month automatically repairs a bad one.

Tax, pension and the self-employed

If you are paid through PAYE, tax is handled — but commission and bonuses can push you into a higher band mid-year, and the tax code catches up later. Check the payslip guide if a deduction looks wrong.

If you invoice clients, nobody is withholding anything for you. Treat tax as a bill, not a surprise:

  • Move a fixed percentage of every invoice into a separate tax account the day it is paid — usually 25–30% of profit for basic-rate self-employment, more if profits are higher.
  • Pay Payments on Account on time; they are based on last year's profit and a low year does not automatically reduce them.
  • Keep the tax account out of your spending view. Money you can see is money you spend.
  • Consider a pension contribution while profits are strong; relief is based on your marginal rate, and the deadline for using an allowance is the tax year, not the month.

When a bad month hits

It will. The plan is not to avoid it but to have a sequence ready:

  1. Draw from the smoothing buffer, not from credit.
  2. Pause the discretionary shares — long-term saving, overpayments — before touching essentials.
  3. Contact priority creditors in the same week, not after the missed date.
  4. Check entitlement: Universal Credit responds to earnings in real time, and a low month can create an award even if a high month did not.
  5. Refill the buffer from the next strong month before restarting overpayments.

This is why an emergency fund matters more on variable pay, not less. One month of essential costs held separately is what stops a quiet January becoming a debt that lasts until summer.

Your checklist

Frequently asked questions

Should I budget on my average or my lowest month?

Your lowest normal month. Essentials must fit that figure. Anything above it is surplus to allocate, which is a much better problem than a shortfall you did not plan for.

How big should the smoothing buffer be?

One month of essential costs is the working target. That is enough to carry a short month without borrowing. Beyond that, money belongs in the emergency fund or debt.

Does Universal Credit work with variable pay?

Yes — it is assessed on actual earnings in each monthly period, so a low month usually produces a higher award. Report changes on time, because late reporting is the main cause of overpayments.

How do I handle a commission payment?

Treat it as income, not a windfall: run it through the same percentages. If you want a rule, take 20% for something enjoyable and allocate the rest — but decide before it lands, not after.

Is a weekly budget better if I am paid weekly?

Often yes. Fixed bills can still be monthly, so keep the smoothing account monthly and budget your variable spending weekly. The mismatch is exactly what the second account is for.
