The SaveGrow money glossary

Every piece of jargon used in our calculators and guides, defined in plain English — with a link to where you'll actually put it to use. Search the list, jump by letter, or just read through; each entry stands on its own.

No term matches that search. Try a shorter word, or ask the SaveGrow assistant on the home page.

A

AER (Annual Equivalent Rate)Savings
The interest rate a savings account would pay in a year if interest were credited once, compounding included. It lets you compare accounts that pay interest monthly, quarterly or annually on a like-for-like basis. Always compare AER against AER — never against the raw “gross” rate. See it in action in the compound interest guide and the interest calculator.
APR (Annual Percentage Rate)Borrowing
The yearly cost of borrowing including most standard fees, used to compare loans, credit cards and mortgages. The opposite side of the coin from AER: higher APR is worse for you because you are the one paying it.
AssetInvesting
Anything you own that holds monetary value — cash, shares, bonds, property, funds. Your asset mix largely determines both your return and how much it can fall.
Automatic transfer / standing orderSaving habits
A scheduled payment you set up once so money moves to savings without a monthly decision. It is the mechanical core of the pay-yourself-first method — willpower fails, direct debits don't.

B

BalanceBasics
The amount currently in an account. Your “available balance” can differ from your “cleared balance” when card payments or cheques are still processing — plan goals from the cleared figure.
BondsInvesting
Loans you make to a government or company, repaid at maturity with regular interest (“coupon”). Generally less volatile than shares but sensitive to interest rates — see the beginner investing guide.
BudgetPlanning
A plan for your income before you spend it, usually split into needs, wants and saving. Frameworks like 50/30/20 are explained in the UK budgeting guide and calculated instantly in the budget tool.

C

Compound interestGrowth
Earning returns on your previous returns as well as on your original money. It is the single most important maths idea in long-term saving — fully worked through in the compound interest guide and modelled in the interest calculator.
ContributionSaving
What you put into a pot or account, as opposed to what it grows into. In the savings goal calculator, target minus contributions equals growth.
Credit scoreBorrowing
A lender's summary of how reliably you've repaid borrowed money. It affects mortgage and loan approvals and pricing; checking it (soft-search style) does not hurt it.
Current accountBanking
Your everyday account for spending, bills and salary. Keeping savings separate from it is one of the simplest friction tricks in the saving guide.

D

DebtBorrowing
Money you owe. Not all debt is equal: a mortgage secures an asset, while high-APR card debt quietly reverses your compounding. Clear expensive debt before aggressive investing.
DiversificationInvesting
Spreading money across many assets, sectors and regions so no single failure is catastrophic. A single global fund achieves a large slice of it in one purchase — see the beginner guide.

E

Emergency fundSafety net
Cash deliberately set aside to cover job loss, repairs or unexpected bills without borrowing. How to size and store one is the whole subject of the UK emergency fund guide; measure yours with the coverage checker.
Essential costsBudgeting
What you must pay to stay housed, fed, healthy and working: rent/mortgage, council tax, utilities, food, travel. They set both your budget's 50% column and your emergency fund's true size.
ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund)Investing
A basket of investments that trades on an exchange like a share. Cheap, transparent diversification — the workhorse vehicle in the beginner guide.

F

Fixed-rate savings accountSavings
An account whose interest rate is locked for a set term in exchange for limited access. Compare fixed versus easy-access trade-offs in the ISA vs savings guide.
FSCS (Financial Services Compensation Scheme)Protection
The UK safety net that protects up to £85,000 per person, per licensed firm if your bank, building society or investment platform fails. Coverage details are on the official FSCS site linked in our guides.
Flexible spendingBudgeting
The “wants” slice of a budget framework (the 30 in 50/30/20). Cuttable quickly when a goal deadline approaches — which is why naming goals matters.

G

Gross vs netIncome
Gross is before tax and deductions; net is what actually lands in your account. Every honest budget starts from net take-home pay, not salary promises.

H

HMRCTax
His Majesty's Revenue and Customs — the UK tax authority. It sets Personal Allowances, ISA limits and savings-interest rules referenced throughout our tax notes.

I

InflationPurchasing power
The general rise in prices, which quietly shrinks what untouched cash buys each year. It is the main argument for putting long-horizon money into growth assets rather than a current account.
InterestBoth directions
The price of money: paid to you on savings, charged by you on borrowing. Our calculator models simple versus compound interest side by side.
ISA (Individual Savings Account)UK tax wrapper
A UK wrapper letting you earn interest, dividends and capital gains free of HMRC tax up to the annual allowance. Cash ISA versus ordinary savings is compared in the ISA vs savings guide.

L

LiquidityAccess
How fast an asset becomes spendable cash without loss. Emergency funds need high liquidity; ten-year investment goals do not.

M

Main residenceProperty
The home you normally live in — relevant to stamp duty, capital gains exemptions and any discussion of property as an investment.
MilestoneGoals
A checkpoint inside a bigger goal (first £500, then one month of costs). Milestones make multi-year plans feel real and are the psychology behind the pace comparison tool.

N

Nominal vs real returnGrowth
Nominal is the number quoted; real subtracts inflation. A 5% account during 7% inflation loses purchasing power — the distinction argued in the compound interest guide.

O

OCF (Ongoing Charges Figure)Fund costs
The yearly percentage cost of holding a fund or ETF, taken inside the investment rather than billed to you. A 0.2% versus 1.5% OCF compounds against you exactly like interest compounds for you.

P

PAYE (Pay As You Earn)Tax
The UK system deducting income tax and National Insurance from salary before you receive it. Budgets should always be built on post-PAYE take-home figures.
Personal AllowanceTax-free
The amount of income you can earn each tax year before paying income tax, plus separate small allowances for savings interest and dividends. Limits change — verify current figures with HMRC.
PortfolioInvesting
The collection of investments you hold. Its allocation — shares, bonds, cash — matters more to long-run outcomes than picking any single winner.

R

Regular saver / direct debit savingsSavings
Accounts designed around a fixed monthly deposit, sometimes with bonus rates. A natural pairing with the savings goal calculator's “monthly → time” mode.
Risk toleranceBehaviour
How much paper loss you can endure without panic-selling. It constrains sensible portfolio design more than any spreadsheet — covered in the beginner guide.

S

Sector fundInvesting
A fund focused on one industry (tech, energy, healthcare). Higher concentration means higher single-sector risk — the opposite of the diversification beginners want first.
Simple interestGrowth
Interest calculated only on the original sum, ignoring accumulated earnings. Rarely used by real products, but our calculator keeps it as a baseline to show what compounding adds.
Stocks and Shares ISAUK wrapper
The ISA variant that wraps investments rather than cash, sheltering gains from tax. Weigh it up in the ISA vs savings guide before opening one.
SubscriptionsSpending
Recurring payments that keep running silently. The quarterly audit in the saving guide treats them as the fastest structural cut available.

T

Take-home payIncome
Net salary after tax, National Insurance and pension deductions — the starting number for every budget we publish.
Target dateGoals
The deadline attached to a saving goal. Goals without dates compete equally against impulse purchases; dated goals win arguments with yourself.
Trial balance / bufferTravel & planning
A percentage added to estimates for surprises — the 10–15% contingency built into the trip budget calculator.

V

Variable rateSavings
An interest rate a bank can change with notice, typical of easy-access accounts. Convenient, but it can fall precisely when you need the income.
VolatilityRisk
The size and speed of an investment's price swings. Volatility is not the same as permanent loss — but it is what tests your risk tolerance.

W

WrapperTax
An account type (ISA, pension) that shelters whatever sits inside it from tax. The wrapper decides the tax treatment; the contents decide the risk.

Y

YieldIncome
Income from an asset as a percentage of its price — bond coupons, dividends, rent. Yield plus growth together form total return.
