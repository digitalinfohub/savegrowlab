The guide behind the tool

How much is actually enough?

Three months of essential costs is the common UK starting point. But “essential” is where people accidentally cheat: the correct figure includes rent, Council Tax, utilities, food, transport to work and minimum debt payments — not your current lifestyle total. For many people the true essentials number is 30–40% lower than normal spending, which makes the target smaller and more achievable than expected.

Six months suits freelancers, commission-based pay, single-income households and anyone with dependants. Twelve months is rare and often means money that should be invested is sitting idle. Size the shield, then move on.

Where to keep it

  • Easy access, separate bank. Same-day or next-day access, but not attached to your debit card.
  • Not investments. The fund must not be able to fall 20% the week you need it.
  • Not a fixed bond. A better rate is not worth a 90-day notice period in a real emergency.
  • Not your current account. Convenience is how emergency funds quietly become spending money.
What counts as an emergency: job loss, a broken boiler, an urgent repair, a vet bill, travel to a sick relative. A sale is not an emergency. Neither is a holiday you forgot to budget for — that is what sinking funds are for.

Building the first £500

The first month is the hardest because the balance is small and the motivation is abstract. Do not wait for a windfall. Set the smallest automatic transfer you will not cancel — £20 a week is £1,040 a year — and leave it alone. Once the balance covers one month of essentials, the psychological effect changes: the account stops looking like spare money and starts looking like a wall.

If you have high-interest debt, split your spare cash: a small starter buffer first, then attack the debt, then return to the full fund. The debt payoff planner shows what overpayments save. For sizing rules and where to hold the money, read the full emergency fund guide.

Frequently asked questions

Is one month of expenses enough?

It is a real start and far better than nothing. Aim for one month first, then three. The target should grow with your responsibilities, not with your optimism.

Should I use a credit card instead?

A card can bridge a timing gap, not a job loss. Interest turns a bad month into a bad year, which is the opposite of what an emergency fund is for.

Where does an ISA fit?

A cash ISA can hold emergency money and shelter the interest from tax. Check access rules — some pay better rates than instant-access accounts.

Do I need six months if I rent?

Renting adds risk: a tenancy ending or a rent rise can cost more than a mortgage surprise. Three months is a floor; six is sensible if your income is variable.
