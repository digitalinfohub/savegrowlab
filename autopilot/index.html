How the rules work

Four rules, in this order

Most money advice fails because it arrives as a list of equally urgent instructions. Saving, investing, overpaying debt and enjoying your pay cannot all be first. The autopilot uses four rules to rank them, and it applies them in the same order every time:

  1. Cover the month. If essentials already eat your take-home pay there is no allocation to make. The plan says so instead of inventing £40 of “fun money”.
  2. Build one month of essentials. A small cash buffer is what stops a car repair becoming a credit card balance. Until it exists, it takes the largest share.
  3. Kill debt above 10% APR. A 22% credit card is a guaranteed 22% return on every pound you overpay, paid in certainty. No savings account competes with that.
  4. Then fund the goal and the long term. Buffer and expensive debt handled, the surplus splits between your named goal, investing and guilt-free spending.

Why the percentages move

The split is not fixed at 50/30/20. It shifts with your situation, which is the entire point of a rule engine rather than a static table:

The plan also gives you a money fitness score out of 100, built from buffer months, debt-to-income, your saving rate and whether a goal exists. It is deliberately blunt: a high score with no goal still caps out.

A worked example

Take £2,600 take-home, £1,900 of essentials, no savings, £2,000 on a card at 22% with a £60 minimum, and a £3,000 goal in 24 months. The surplus is £700. Because the buffer is empty and the card is expensive, the rules push most of the money there first: roughly £333 to the buffer, £200 extra to the card, £100 to the goal, with about £67 left as spending money. The goal is then funded at £100 a month, which takes 30 months — six months past the deadline.

That mismatch is the useful output, not a failure. You now have three honest choices: move the deadline, raise the goal contribution, or accept a slower buffer. What you cannot do is fund all three at full speed, and pretending otherwise is how budgets die.

How to make it stick

  • Do the standing order on payday, not later. Money that survives the weekend is money you have already decided to keep.
  • Separate the pots. Buffer, goal and spending should not share an account with your debit card.
  • Tick the weekly moves. The five tasks below the plan are stored on this device. Finishing them is the difference between a plan and a screenshot.
  • Rerun it when life changes. A pay rise, a move, a new baby or a cleared card all change the weights. Recalculating takes ten seconds.
When to ignore the plan: if essentials exceed 70% of take-home, the fix is structural — rent, transport, childcare, income — not a better spreadsheet. Use the budgeting guide and the income guide instead of shaving £5 off groceries.

Questions about the autopilot

What counts as an essential cost?

Rent or mortgage, Council Tax, energy, water, insurance, food, transport to work and minimum debt payments. Streaming, takeaways, gym and holidays are not essentials — they belong in the spending line. Be honest rather than aspirational: an understated essentials figure produces a plan that cannot be kept.

Why does the plan pay debt before investing?

Because a 22% credit card is a guaranteed 22% return on every overpayment, while an investment return is uncertain. Once the rate is below roughly 10% and the buffer exists, the tool switches to funding the goal and investing alongside the debt.

Does it store my income?

Only if you tick a task: the checklist state is kept in your browser's local storage. The numbers themselves are recalculated in the page and never sent anywhere. Clearing site data removes everything.

Why is my goal behind schedule?

Usually because the buffer or expensive debt is taking priority, which is deliberate. The plan shows the honest month count. Move the deadline, raise the goal contribution, or accept the trade-off — those are the only three levers.

Is this financial advice?

No. It is arithmetic and a set of documented rules. For advice about your circumstances, speak to a regulated adviser; for free debt help, contact StepChange, National Debtline or MoneyHelper.
