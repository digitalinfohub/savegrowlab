Investing starter

What should I invest in — and what will it cost?

Five questions score your risk tolerance, then the tool turns it into a plain asset mix, a range of outcomes, and the one number most beginners ignore: the annual fee. Education, not a recommendation.

The thinking behind the tool

Risk is a time horizon problem first

The five questions do not measure courage. They measure two things that actually matter: how long the money can stay invested and whether you have a cash buffer so you never have to sell at the wrong moment. Someone with a ten-year horizon and six months of expenses can hold a volatile portfolio through a bad year. Someone investing next year's house deposit cannot, no matter how relaxed they feel today.

That is why the tool will happily tell you that a 3-year horizon is a cash question, not an investing one. It is not being cautious for its own sake: a 20% fall in year three of a five-year plan needs a 25% rise just to get back to level, and you may not have the time.

What the three buckets are

  • Global shares (equities). Part-ownership of thousands of companies. Highest expected long-run return, widest short-run swings. A single global tracker fund is the usual beginner building block.
  • Bonds and gilts. Loans to governments and companies. Usually steadier, lower expected return, and they can still fall when interest rates rise.
  • Cash. Not really an investment — the part of the pot you may need soon, or the part that lets you sleep. It loses purchasing power to inflation over long periods.

The percentages the tool returns are a starting point for a conversation with yourself, not a prescription. A common beginner mistake is holding 100% shares because the long-run number looks best, then selling in the first 20% drawdown. The right mix is the most adventurous one you will actually keep through a bad year.

The fee line is the controllable part

Returns are uncertain. Fees are contractual. Two costs matter most: the platform charge (a percentage of your pot, sometimes a flat fee) and the fund's ongoing charge. Add them together for the number this tool asks for. On a £10,000 pot growing at 6% before fees for 20 years, paying 0.2% instead of 1.2% leaves roughly £15,600 more in the pot — and that is before any exit charges. The maths is in the fees guide.

Watch for the fee that hides: a fund that consistently underperforms its index is also a cost, just an invisible one. That is the argument for low-cost trackers as a default, not because active management can never work, but because picking the winner in advance is hard and the fee is certain.

A sane first month

  1. Check your workplace pension and capture every pound of employer match before anything else — it is an instant, guaranteed return.
  2. Size the cash buffer with the emergency fund calculator.
  3. Clear any debt above roughly 10% APR. The autopilot ranks this for you.
  4. Open one low-cost ISA, choose one diversified global fund, and set a standing order for an amount you could keep paying in a bad month.
  5. Then do nothing for a year except keep contributing. Boring is the strategy.
What this tool cannot do: it does not know your tax position, your pension, your partner's finances or your job security, and it does not recommend a specific product. It shows the arithmetic of a mix so you can ask better questions.

Investing starter questions

Is this a recommendation?

No. The tool scores your answers and shows a generic asset mix with illustrative returns. It does not know your tax position, pension, dependants or job security, and it cannot tell you which specific fund to buy.

Why show three columns?

Because a single number implies precision that investing does not have. The pessimistic and optimistic columns are the same mix with a 2.5 percentage point lower or higher return. Real annual returns scatter around the middle and can be negative for years.

What fee should I enter?

Add the platform fee to the fund's ongoing charge. A mainstream app plus a global tracker is often 0.2–0.5% all-in; a managed service can be 0.8–1.5% or more. Check the actual documents rather than the marketing page.

Should I use a pension or an ISA?

Pensions win on tax relief and employer contributions but lock the money away. ISAs are flexible and tax-free but get no relief. Most people should capture the full employer match first, then use an ISA for goals before retirement age.

What if I need the money sooner?

Then it probably should not be invested. Cash savings and premium bonds do not fall in value. The tool flags horizons under five years for that reason.
