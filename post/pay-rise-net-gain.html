Your £2,000 pay rise is not £2,000

A raise is quoted gross, annual and before pension. What lands in your account is none of those things. Knowing the gap before the letter arrives stops the disappointment — and stops the lifestyle creep that swallows the rest.

SaveGrow Lab19 November 20267 min read

Suppose your salary rises from £32,000 to £34,000. The increase is £2,000 a year, which sounds like £166 a month. Then payroll runs it and you see rather less. Three things happen in order, and each one takes a bite.

Step one: income tax

Above the personal allowance, most earned income in England, Wales and Northern Ireland is taxed at 20% in the basic band, 40% above the higher-rate threshold, and 45% above the additional threshold. Scotland has its own bands and different thresholds. The marginal rate — the rate on the next pound — is what matters for a raise, not your average rate.

On a straightforward basic-rate rise, £2,000 of extra salary attracts about £400 of income tax. Move from basic into higher rate and the marginal rate on that slice doubles.

Step two: National Insurance

Employee National Insurance is charged on earnings above the primary threshold, at a percentage that has changed several times in recent years. Whatever the current rate is, it applies to the marginal pound, so a raise is also an NI event. For a basic-rate employee, tax and NI together typically take a bit under a third of an increase.

Step three: pension and student loan

This is where the answer diverges by person:

  • Pension contributions. If you contribute a percentage of salary, the contribution rises with the raise. That reduces take-home but increases the amount saved — and if it is salary sacrifice, it also reduces the NI bill.
  • Student loan. Plan 1, 2, 4 and 5, and postgraduate loans, all take a percentage above a threshold. A raise increases the repayment, so the net gain is smaller than the tax calculation alone suggests.
  • Benefit and tax-credit interactions. If you receive Universal Credit, a raise reduces the award through the taper. The effective marginal rate can be far higher than the tax rate, which is why "earn more and be no better off" is a real experience rather than a myth.
  • Childcare and free hours. Some schemes have cliff edges at earnings thresholds. Crossing one can cost more than the raise adds — check before you accept a specific figure.

The arithmetic, roughly

StepEffect on a £2,000 riseRunning total
Gross increase+£2,000£2,000
Income tax at 20%−£400£1,600
National Insurance (illustrative)−£160£1,440
Pension at 5% of the increase−£100£1,340
Student loan, Plan 2, if applicable−£180£1,160

These are illustration figures, not your payroll. Rates and thresholds change, Scotland differs, and pension method matters. The reliable way to know is to put your actual numbers into a take-home calculator or to ask payroll for a projection — most will run one if you ask before accepting an offer.

Why the disappointment matters

People plan the raise before it arrives. A holiday, a car payment, a slightly better flat. When the actual figure is 25–40% lower, the plan either gets funded from somewhere else or becomes debt. That is the mechanism behind the familiar story of earning more and feeling no better off.

The decision to make before payday

Decide what happens to the net increase before the first payment lands. A rule that works for most people:

  • Half to the boring stuff — emergency fund, debt overpayment, or pension.
  • Half to your life — deliberately, on something you choose, so the raise is not invisible.

Without a rule, the default is that the whole increase dissolves into slightly nicer groceries and one more subscription. That is not a character flaw; it is just what happens when money arrives without a job.
