The one-evening spending audit that finds £200

You do not need a spreadsheet, an app subscription or a personality change. You need one hour, five passes over your statements, and the discipline to move the difference before it disappears.

SaveGrow Lab Editorial Team11 November 20267 min read

In short

  • One hour, five passes, no spreadsheet. Annualise everything so small charges look their real size.
  • The biggest leaks are usually fixed costs and interest, not lattes.
  • Cancel one thing, renegotiate one thing, and automate the saving of the difference.

Set a timer, not a mood

Sixty minutes, phone on silent, statements open. The goal is not a perfect budget — it is to find three costs you did not consciously choose. Work in passes, because switching between “bills” and “fun” is how the hour disappears.

Pass 1: fixed costs (15 minutes)

List rent or mortgage, Council Tax, energy, water, broadband, mobile, insurance, childcare and transport. For each, ask two questions: is this the cheapest equivalent, and am I on a rate that has quietly expired?

  • Energy: check the current tariff against your supplier's available tariffs and a comparison site. A fixed deal that ended months ago can be the most expensive option in the house.
  • Broadband and mobile: out-of-contract prices are usually the worst prices. A five-minute call often produces a retention offer.
  • Insurance: renewals are priced for inertia. Compare before the auto-renew date, not after.

Pass 2: subscriptions (10 minutes)

Search your statements for recurring card payments and direct debits. Include the ones you forgot: cloud storage, a fitness app, a news site, a gaming pass, a domain you do not use. Annualise each one — a £7.99 monthly charge is £95.88 a year.

Sort into three piles: keep, pause, cancel. Cancel the ones you would not buy again today at full price. Pause the ones you use seasonally. Do not cancel something that genuinely improves your week just to feel virtuous; that is how budgets get abandoned.

Pass 3: the variable shop (15 minutes)

Look at the last two months of groceries, eating out and transport. Do not judge — look for patterns. The usual finding is not “too many coffees” but “three top-up shops a week because the meal plan does not exist”.

One structural fix beats ten small deprivations: a weekly shop with a list, a lunch that is planned the night before, or a route that avoids the expensive convenience store.

Pass 4: leaks and fees (10 minutes)

LeakAnnual cost if ignored
Credit card interest at 22% on £1,500About £330 a year if the balance does not fall
Overdraft fees at £5 a month£60
Unused gym at £32 a month£384
Two streaming services at £10.99 each£263.76
Out-of-contract broadband at £42 vs £28£168
Cash ISA at 0.5% vs 4% on £8,000£280 of interest foregone

The savings account line is the one people miss. Money sitting in a legacy account earning almost nothing is a cost, even though no payment leaves your account.

Pass 5: annualise and decide (10 minutes)

Add the monthly leaks and multiply by 12. Most people find £100–£300 a month that was not doing anything deliberate. Now choose three actions, not ten:

  1. Cancel one recurring cost.
  2. Renegotiate or switch one fixed bill.
  3. Move the monthly difference to a separate savings pot by standing order, on the day after payday.

If you skip the third action, the money will be absorbed by ordinary life within a month. The audit is not the win; the transfer is.

How much can a spending audit realistically find?

For most households, £100–£300 a month in cancellations, renegotiated bills and unnecessary interest. The exact figure depends on how long it has been since anything was reviewed.

Should I cancel everything I enjoy?

No. A plan with no pleasure is abandoned. Cut the things you do not use or would not rebuy, keep the ones that genuinely improve your life.

What if my partner does not want to audit?

Do your own accounts first. A visible saving is more persuasive than an argument about budgeting.
