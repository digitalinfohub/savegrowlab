What £100 a month invested actually becomes

Small monthly amounts are not a compromise. They are the whole mechanism. Here are the 10, 20 and 30-year numbers, the fee that quietly removes five figures, and the order to do things in.

SaveGrow Lab Editorial Team17 November 20268 min read

In short

  • £100 a month at 6% for 20 years is about £46,200, of which £24,000 is your money.
  • The rate matters less than the time and the fee. A 1% charge can remove five figures over 20 years.
  • Put the £100 in a low-cost global tracker inside an ISA — after the buffer and any expensive debt.

The numbers, before the opinions

Assume you invest £100 at the end of every month. No lump sum, no clever timing, no withdrawals. Here is what the pot is worth at three different average annual returns:

YearsYou put inAt 4%At 6%At 8%
10£12,000£14,725£16,388£18,295
20£24,000£36,677£46,204£58,902
30£36,000£69,405£100,452£149,036

Three things are worth noticing. First, the gap between 4% and 8% is enormous over 30 years — assumptions do a lot of work, which is why you should plan on the pessimistic column. Second, most of the 30-year total is growth, not contributions. Third, none of these numbers happen if you stop in year four.

These are fixed-rate projections, not forecasts. Real returns arrive out of order, with falls in the middle. A 20% drop in year 12 is normal and does not break a 30-year plan; selling during it does.

What £100 actually buys

One global index fund. Not five. A single fund holding thousands of companies across developed and emerging markets gives you diversification in one line, usually for 0.05–0.25% a year. Add the platform charge and your all-in cost should land between 0.2% and 0.5%.

You do not need to pick stocks to start. In fact, picking stocks before you understand position sizing is the most reliable way for a beginner to lose money while feeling busy.

The fee line

On a £10,000 pot growing at 6% before fees for 20 years, paying 1.2% instead of 0.2% leaves about £15,600 less at the end. The fee is small each year and enormous over time because it compounds against you. The full working is in the fees guide.

The order of operations

  1. One month of essentials in cash. Without it, the first emergency forces a sale or a credit card.
  2. Any debt above roughly 10% APR. A 22% card is a guaranteed 22% return.
  3. Your full employer pension match. Free money, before any investment decision.
  4. Then the £100 a month, in a Stocks & Shares ISA if the goal is before retirement.

If you follow that order, the £100 is genuinely long-term money. If you skip to step four, the first bad month can undo years of contributions.

Four ways people ruin it

  • Tinkering. Switching funds after every headline. The fund was not the problem; the reaction was.
  • Stopping. A pause of six months costs more than a fee increase, because the missing contributions never compound.
  • Panic selling. Locking in the fall and then waiting for “confidence” to return, usually after the recovery.
  • Adding a speculative sleeve too early. Crypto or single stocks at 30% of a beginner portfolio is not diversification; it is a second, worse portfolio.

Is £100 a month enough to bother?

Yes. It is enough to build the habit, enough to learn how you react to a fall, and enough to compound into a meaningful pot over decades. Starting small and continuing beats starting big and stopping.

Which fund should I buy?

A single low-cost global equity tracker is the common starting point. This site does not recommend specific products; compare ongoing charges and check the key information document.

What return should I assume?

Plan on 4–5% after fees for a global equity mix, and check that the plan still works at 3%. Anything above that is optimism, not a plan.
