You got £500. Here is the order to spend it in.

Money that arrives unexpectedly behaves differently from money you earned. It feels like a bonus, so it gets spent like one — usually in a fortnight, usually without a single memorable purchase. A decision rule fixes that in about ten minutes.

SaveGrow Lab Editorial TeamUpdated 19 November 2026

The short version

  • Cover the next 30 days first — that is the difference between a windfall and a loan.
  • Then the most expensive debt, then the emergency fund, in that order.
  • Spend about 10% deliberately, so the remaining 90% is not quietly absorbed.
  • Invest last, not first. £500 is a buffer before it is a portfolio.

Why windfalls vanish

A salary is allocated before it arrives: rent, bills, travel, food. A windfall has no allocation, so it floats. Floating money gets spent on things that do not show up in any budget line — a bigger shop, a round, a taxi, a "treat" you cannot name a month later.

The fix is not discipline, it is a sequence. Decide the order once, apply it every time money arrives unexpectedly, and the decision stops being a negotiation with yourself in a shop.

The order

1. Cover the next 30 days

Before anything else, make sure the month is funded: rent, energy, food, travel, the direct debits due before your next payday. This is not saving, it is removing the reason you would otherwise borrow. If £500 arrives three days before payday and your account is at £40, the windfall has already been spent — you just have not noticed.

2. Kill the most expensive debt

Compare interest rates, not balances. A credit card or overdraft charging more than roughly 8% a year is a guaranteed loss that grows faster than any savings account pays. £500 against a 24% card saves about £120 a year in interest, with no risk and no product to choose.

If you have several debts, put the windfall on the highest rate — the avalanche — unless you need the psychological win of clearing a small balance entirely. Both work; the second is often the one people finish.

3. Top up the emergency fund

Once nothing is charging you 20% and the month is funded, the money belongs in cash. The target is three months of essential spending, but the first £500 does something specific: it turns most emergencies into an inconvenience. A boiler repair, a tyre, a dental bill — the events that usually go on a card.

Keep it somewhere separate from your current account, ideally not attached to your debit card. A pot you have to make two transfers to reach is a pot you do not raid for a night out.

4. Spend 10% on purpose

Deliberate spending is not a weakness in the plan, it is part of it. £50 of a £500 windfall, decided in advance, prevents the low-grade resentment that leads to a £300 "I deserve it" moment three weeks later. Buy something you will still be able to name in a year.

5. Automate whatever is left

Move the remainder the same day. Money that stays in the current account until "the right time" gets reclassified as spending money by the end of the week. A standing order to the savings pot, set for the day after the windfall lands, is the whole trick.

Why not invest it?

Because £500 invested while you have no buffer is a bet that nothing breaks. Something usually breaks. Selling investments in a bad month to fix a car converts a temporary paper loss into a permanent one, and it teaches you that investing does not work.

Once the buffer exists and expensive debt is gone, investing a windfall becomes sensible — a Stocks and Shares ISA, a low-cost global fund, and the same automation rule. The order matters more than the product.

One rule that makes it stick

Write the split down before the money arrives, not after. "Any unexpected money: month first, then the card, then the pot, then 10% to spend." Four lines in a notes app is enough. The point is not the precision — it is that the decision is already made when the money is in front of you.
