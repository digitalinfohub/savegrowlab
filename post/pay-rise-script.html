The pay-rise conversation, word for word

Most raises are decided before the meeting: by the evidence you bring, the number you say and the timing you choose. Here is a script that survives a difficult manager and a tight budget.

SaveGrow Lab Editorial Team14 November 20266 min read

In short

  • Ask when budgets are being set, with a number and three evidenced outcomes.
  • Frame it as a business case, not a personal need — but bring the personal consequence if the answer is no.
  • Get the outcome, the reason and the next review date in writing, whatever the answer.

Before the meeting

Most pay conversations are lost in preparation, not delivery. You need four things: your current scope, three outcomes with numbers, a market range, and a specific ask.

EvidenceWeak versionStrong version
Scope“I work hard.”“I now own the supplier reporting and two client accounts added in January.”
Outcome“The team relies on me.”“I cut the monthly close from six days to three and recovered £18,000 of unbilled work.”
Market“I saw a job paying more.”“Three comparable roles are advertised at £38,000–£42,000.”
Ask“Can we look at my salary?”“I am asking for £40,000 from 1 April.”

Bring the range, not a fantasy number. If the market says £36,000–£40,000 and you ask for £48,000, the conversation becomes about the gap rather than your case.

Timing

Ask a few months before the end of the financial year, or immediately after a visible win. Avoid the week of a redundancy announcement, the day after a missed target, or a Friday afternoon. Ask for a dedicated 20 minutes rather than ambushing someone after a meeting.

The script

“Thanks for making time. I want to talk about my salary. Since March I have taken on the supplier reporting and the two new client accounts, and the monthly close is now three days instead of six. Comparable roles are advertised at £38,000–£42,000, and I am asking for £40,000 from April. I would like to understand what is possible.”

Then stop talking. The silence is uncomfortable and it is doing work for you. Let them respond to the number rather than filling the gap with reasons why you deserve less.

The four answers

  • “Yes.” Ask for it in writing with the effective date. Confirm in an email the same day.
  • “We can do £37,000.” Accept, decline, or trade: “I can work with £37,000 if we agree a review to £40,000 in six months against these two criteria.”
  • “Not now.” Ask for a date and the specific criteria in writing. “What would need to be true by September?” A vague no is not an answer.
  • “We do not have budget.” Ask whether the constraint is the role or the budget, and what would change it. Start looking. That is information, not a threat.

The follow-up email

Send it within an hour, whatever happened:

“Thanks for the conversation. To confirm: I asked for £40,000 from 1 April, based on the expanded scope and the market range we discussed. You mentioned [outcome]. Could you confirm the next step and the date we will revisit this? Happy to put together anything else that would help.”

This is not aggression. It is a record. Managers change, budgets change, and a written target is much harder to forget than a verbal one.

If you decide to move

Price the move properly: commuting, childcare, pension, holiday, probation and the value of flexibility. A 15% rise can be a real-terms cut. And do not use a counter-offer as a reason to stay unless you would have stayed at the same money — otherwise you have simply delayed the decision.

Your preparation checklist

Pay-rise prep
What if my manager says there is a pay freeze?

Ask what the process is for exceptions and when the freeze is reviewed. Get the date. If there is genuinely no route, that is a strong signal to test the market.

Should I mention another job offer?

Only if it is real and you are prepared to take it. Bluffing is a one-time move that damages trust permanently.

How much should I ask for?

Anchor to the market range for your role and region, then add your evidenced scope. A 5–15% ask is typical; a 30% ask needs a role change to justify it.
